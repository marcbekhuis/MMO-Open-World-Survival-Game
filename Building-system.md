# Building System

Building is how a player stakes a claim on the world: a lean-to against the rain on the first night, a timber hall with a forge and a stable yard a season later, a stone keep that a whole guild bleeds to hold. The system works the way the genre's best piece-based builders do, in the tradition of [ARK: Survival Evolved](https://ark.wiki.gg/wiki/Building) and [Conan Exiles](https://www.conanexiles.com/): structures are made of crafted pieces that snap together, each piece has to be carried by something beneath it, and anything that stands can be burned, battered, or brought down. This document covers how pieces are made and placed, how structures stand and fall, how land is claimed and kept, and how the same pieces become a home for tamed creatures.

This document is the conceptual design. Its implementation in Unreal Engine 5 — the piece records and snap points, the stability graph, damage and claims, and the replication and persistence of player-built structures — is set out in [Building System (Technical)](<Building-system (Technical).md>).

## One System for Everyone

All humanoid structures, player-built or NPC-built, use the same construction system. Architecture is part of the shared world language: players build in human, elven, or dwarven styles or mix them into hybrid settlements, they understand an NPC town through the rules that govern their own walls, and they attack or defend any building in a raid without a separate ruleset. The NPC settlements laid out by [Settlement Generation](Settlement-generation.md) are made of the same pieces, from the same materials, and fail in the same ways.

## Crafting and Carrying Pieces

Nothing is built straight from raw materials. Every piece is crafted first, at the station that works its material under [Crafting](Crafting.md): a carpenter's bench turns timber into wall panels, posts, and floor beams, and into window frames, shutters, and door leaves; a mason's yard dresses stone into blocks, foundation slabs, sills, and lintels; a smithy forges the iron bands, hinges, and window bars of reinforced work; and a glassworker's furnace leads small panes of glass into window lights. The result is an item, a wall or a foundation or a shutter, which goes into the builder's inventory and is placed from there.

Crafted pieces keep their weight. A thatch panel is light, a timber wall is a load, and a dressed stone foundation is a burden one character can barely carry, under the same weight rules as everything else in [Inventory](Inventory.md). Where a base stands therefore decides how hard it is to build. Close to the forests and quarries, with workshops at hand, a stone hall goes up quickly. Out on the frontier every heavy piece has to come by pack beast, cart, or ship, or be made on the spot. A field bench set up on site makes simple timber and thatch pieces from local wood and grass, but stone and reinforced work need proper stations, so a frontier stronghold either hauls its walls in one piece at a time or builds its own workshops first. A caravan of building stone is a valuable and very slow target.

## Placing Pieces

Building is meant to feel refined: quick to rough out, precise to finish, and generous with options at every step. Structural pieces snap together, and the snapping is the heart of it. Square and triangular variants let a builder turn corners, ring a tower, or follow a cliff edge. Every foundation must touch the ground: it is refused wherever the earth under its four corners and its centre falls away further than its skirt can hide, so a foundation never hangs off a ledge. On a gentle slope each foundation settles into the hillside on its own, stepping down from its neighbour. On a steep one the builder sets pillars into the ground and lays floors across them to make a level surface. A free-standing foundation can also be raised up to a metre above flush in steps of ten centimetres, its skirt reaching down to the earth, which lifts a floor clear of wet ground or levels a terrace, and anything snapped to it inherits the height. Pieces cannot be placed over resource nodes, on the roads that link settlements, or inside the protected ground around NPC settlements and the [great cities](Structures/Great-cities.md).

Every piece is one simple part. A wall is the wall itself, boards or plaster or coursed stone with an opening where it has one, and everything that dresses it is a piece of its own: the posts and crossbeams of a half-timbered face, the frame set into a window, the glass in that frame, the shutters that close over it, the sill beneath it, the door in a doorway. Nothing is baked into anything else, except what makes a material what it is: the iron bands of reinforced stone and the crystal veins of warded stone are part of every piece of those materials, because without them the wall is simply a lower material. Everything optional is separate, so two builders who start from the same plain wall can finish it as a frontier cabin, a timber-framed town house, or a barred guardhouse, and no look the game supports is out of reach because a part came welded to the wrong piece. [Settlement Generation](Settlement-generation.md) builds every NPC house from the same parts, so a town's character comes from how its builders combined them, not from pieces only NPCs can have.

The core families of structural piece are these:

- **Foundations**, square and triangular, in full and half widths, which take their support from the ground and are, with wall foundations and pillars, the only pieces that can.
- **Wall foundations**, ground-supported like any foundation but only as thick as a wall, which line the outer edge of an ordinary foundation so that the wall stands beside the floor rather than on top of it. A room ringed with them keeps every square of its floor inside, and on open ground they carry fences and palisades.
- **Pillars**, which carry floors and roofs up from the ground, or up from a foundation or ceiling as ordinary stacked joints.
- **Walls**, solid or with an opening, at full width and at half width so a facade need not march in three-metre bays, and at full and half height. An opening is cut for a window, a large window, a pair of windows, a door, a double door, a gate, or a hatch, and sits to the left, in the centre, or to the right of its wall, so a street of houses built from the same kit never lines its windows up like a barracks. The opening is a bare hole in the wall; what fills it is a fitting.
- **Floors and ceilings**, in full and half widths, which are the same piece seen from either side.
- **Roofs**, which also decide whether the space below counts as covered. Every roof material comes in three pitches, 27, 45, and 60 degrees, and as a flat roof, with gable ends and ridges to match each, so a thatched hall can sit low and a slate tower can rise to a needle; some materials read best at one of them, as thatch does steep and adobe flat, but none is limited to it.
- **Stairs, ramps, and ladders**, which join one level to the next.
- **Frames and beams**, the posts, crossbeams, braces, and lintels that fix onto the face of a wall, floor, or ceiling and carry load like any other structural piece.

Beside these families stand the **fittings**, which dress and close the structure without holding it up. They are described under [Fittings](#fittings) below.

### Snap Points

Every piece carries many snap points, not one per edge, and the builder can always see them. As soon as a piece is in hand, every valid snap point on the nearby structure lights up as a small marker, the one the preview will lock to is highlighted, and the builder can step through the other candidates at the same spot before committing. Each marker is drawn just outside the piece it belongs to, as a small arrow along the joint it would form, so that a point never vanishes inside the post or wall it sits on, and the highlighted point is named on the screen, as [UI and HUD](UI-and-HUD.md#building-and-snap-markers) describes. Foundations join edge to edge and corner to corner, walls stand on foundation edges or on wall foundations, ceilings rest on walls and pillars, roofs cap walls, and stairs and ramps hook onto floors.

Pillars are where the extra options matter most. A wall can meet a pillar centred on the pillar, so that the wall's end runs into the middle of the post, or flush with either of its outer corners, so that the pillar stands proud of the wall or sits hidden behind it. The same choice applies at every face of the pillar, which lets one post anchor a straight run, a corner, or a junction of walls meeting from three or four sides, and the three positions on a face are marked in different colours so the choice is never a guess. Wall faces are just as generous. Posts, crossbeams, diagonal braces, and lintels snap onto any face of any wall, at its edges, its middle, or its quarter lines, so a builder can dress a plain wall as half-timbered work, brace a long run, or band a stone tower with timber without any of it floating free. Braces and crossbeams are structural, not only decorative: they pass support along like any other piece, with small losses of their own, so bracing a long run is a choice about strength as well as looks.

### Clean Joints

Pieces never fight one another visually. Wherever two pieces meet or overlap, such as a wall running into a pillar, two walls crossing at a corner, a beam laid across a wall face, or a floor tucked under a wall foundation, the joint resolves cleanly instead of showing two surfaces flickering through each other. The system does this in two layers. First, each piece reads its neighbours and swaps to the model variant that fits the joint: a corner piece where two walls meet, a T-junction where a third arrives, an end cap where a run stops, a notched beam where it crosses a post. Second, where pieces still overlap, one surface always takes priority and the other is hidden behind it, so even an unusual arrangement never clips. A builder can therefore set pieces as close together as the snap points allow and trust the result to look built rather than stacked.

Joints are worked out within a material. Where two different materials meet, a timber wall standing on a stone one or a brick gable against a plastered side, the edge stays a plain, hard edge, as it does in the genre's familiar builders, and nothing flickers, because the overlap rule still holds. A builder who wants the seam hidden dresses it the way a real one would, with a post or pillar on the corner, a sill beam along the join, or a trim across it.

### Fittings

Fittings are the parts that fill an opening or dress a face: window frames, the leaded glass set into them, shutters, sills, iron bars and grilles, door and gate leaves, hatches, bargeboards along a gable, carved or painted trims, and the like. Each is its own crafted piece, made of its own material, so oak shutters can hang on a stone wall and an iron grille can bar a timber window. A fitting needs a host. It snaps into the sockets of an opening, the frame inside the hole, the glass inside the frame, the shutters on the frame's hinge edges, the sill below, or onto the snap lines of a wall face for a trim, and it cannot be placed where there is nothing to hold it.

Fittings carry no load and take no part in stability. They never hold a wall up, cost nothing from the stability of the piece they hang on, and can be added, swapped, or taken down without touching anything around them, which is what makes it safe to dress a building freely. When their host falls, they fall with it and drop as damaged items, the way a chest drops from a collapsed floor.

What fills an opening decides what the opening does, so the choice is never only about looks. A bare window lets in light, weather, and anyone who can reach it. Glass keeps out rain and cold while letting in light, and breaks at the first blow. Shutters close an opening against weather and arrows, open from the inside, and can be barred; iron bars stop a body but not a spear thrust or an arrow. A door leaf is what makes a doorway a door. A builder who wants a bright hall and a safe one puts glass behind bars and shutters inside the glass, and a raider who studies a wall reads its openings before its stones.

Inside a claim, doors, gates, hatches, shutters, and containers open only for the claim's owner and for those granted access under the claim's permissions. Everyone else must break them or go around, so a base is closed the moment its owners log out. Outside any claim a door or a chest opens for anyone in reach. Barring is something else: it is what a person inside does on top of that rule, setting a bar across a door or shutter that then has to be broken even by someone with access. The world has no lock items, no keys, and no lockpicking, because the claim is the lock.

### Decoration and Detail

Everything that is not structure or fitting, such as crafting stations, storage chests, beds, hearths, feeding troughs, furniture, and decoration, is placed freely rather than snapped. It sits on floors, tables, and shelves, hangs on walls and from beams, and can be rotated in fine steps, nudged by small distances, aligned to the surface beneath it, or set down on an optional light grid when a row of things needs to line up.

A placed piece can also change its finish in place, without tearing it down and without touching its strength or the support it gives its neighbours. The finish is the surface of the same part: a timber wall left rough, planed smooth, or chip-carved; a stone wall with a pitched, tooled, or rubbed face; plaster limewashed white or tinted. Where a wall type has two faces worth telling apart, its finishes include face combinations: a plaster wall can show plaster on one side and stone on the other, plaster on both, or stone on both, so a room can be rendered and limewashed inside a stone shell without a single extra piece. A finish never adds a part. Anything that adds one, a frame, a moulding, a bargeboard, a shutter, is a fitting or a structural piece placed in its own right. Finishing a building is as much a part of the craft as raising it.

### Patterns

Building from simple parts would be slow if every window had to be assembled one part at a time, so a builder can save any group of placed pieces as a pattern: a glazed and shuttered window, a half-timbered wall bay, a gatehouse front, a whole cottage. A pattern records which pieces it holds and where each sits relative to the others, and placing it shows the whole group as one preview that snaps like a single piece. Every piece in it is still a crafted item from the builder's inventory, still checked for support and claim rights, and still placed from the bottom up, so a pattern saves effort, never materials. Pieces the builder does not carry stay as faint outlines in place, to be filled in as they are made.

Patterns can be named, kept, and passed on. A builder can write one out as a plan, an item another player reads to learn it, so a guild's house style, a famous mason's gatehouse, or a well-tested defensive window travel the frontier the way recipes do, and good plans are worth trading. The styles of NPC towns are libraries of the same patterns, which is how [Settlement Generation](Settlement-generation.md) dresses a street in human, elven, or dwarven work from the parts a player can build with.


## Materials

Building materials are not a short ladder of tiers. They form an open family that grows with the world, and every material is defined by the same handful of properties: how much punishment a piece can take, how far it carries support, how it resists each kind of damage, how well it keeps out cold and heat, how it weathers, and how much it weighs and costs. Thatch and hide go up fast and burn faster. Rough timber is the frontier's backbone, and planked and treated timber resist rot and weather better. Clay, adobe, and fired brick keep a building cool in the [Scorching Desert](Biomes/Scorching-desert.md). Fieldstone and dressed stone shrug off axes and fire. Between them sits cobblestone, the first mortared stone: rounded river and field cobbles, roughly fist- to head-sized, laid in coursed rows in lime mortar, a step between dry-laid fieldstone and dressed stone and used for foundations, lower walls, and paving. Iron-banded reinforced stone is the material of keeps. How each material looks in the hand and at eye level is set out in the [Building Materials](Art-direction/Building-materials.md) briefs. Each people brings its own variations, such as elven work in living, shaped wood, dwarven masonry cut to fit without mortar, and the timber-and-plaster of human towns, and rare materials join late in a character's career, including stone laced with [mana crystal](Glossary.md#creatures-and-magic) for warded walls.

Upgrading means replacing. A builder who wants stone where timber stood crafts the stone piece, removes the timber one, recovering part of its materials, and sets the new piece in the empty socket. The fittings on the old piece come off whole into the builder's inventory and go back onto the new one. Upgrading a wall is therefore a moment of weakness. For as long as the gap stands open, the base has a hole in it, and the support that piece gave its neighbours is gone, so a careful builder upgrades from the inside out and never with raiders in sight.

## Stability and Support

Every piece has a stability value, and nothing stands without it. A foundation resting on the ground has full stability, and so does a pillar whose foot stands on the earth itself. A pillar standing on a foundation or a ceiling is only another stacked joint, so a tower cannot restart its support at full strength halfway up and rise without limit. Every piece joined to them takes a share of that stability, losing a little with each connection away from the nearest support. A wall on a foundation is almost as stable as the foundation itself, a ceiling one span out from the wall is less so, and a balcony three spans beyond is weaker still. When a piece would fall to zero, it cannot be placed. Stacking straight up costs little stability and reaching out sideways costs a great deal, so tall towers are cheap to raise and long overhangs need pillars beneath them.

Each material carries support in its own way. Stone stands tall but spans poorly without support beneath it, while timber spans further but cannot rise as high, and reinforced stone does both well. A builder sees all of this as they work. The placement preview colours each piece by its stability, shading from blue through green and yellow to red as support thins out, and a builder's hammer shows the same colours on anything already built. A structure's weak points are therefore visible to its owner and, at a glance, to anyone planning to bring it down.

Stability is recalculated only around a piece that is placed, removed, or destroyed, never across a whole base at once, which keeps large settlements cheap for the servers to hold. When a piece is destroyed, every piece that depended on it loses support. Anything that drops to zero collapses, and the falling rubble damages whatever lies beneath it. Raiders who understand this go for the pillars rather than the walls. A chest, bed, or trough that stood on a piece that falls drops as a damaged item, and whatever it held spills into a pile that anyone may loot.

### A Wall Raised Step by Step

A builder on a gentle slope sets a dressed-stone foundation into the hillside. The preview settles into the ground, glowing blue: it is touching earth and has full stability. A second foundation snaps to its downhill edge and steps down the slope on its own skirt, settling into the earth a little lower than the first, and it glows blue as well, since every foundation stands on the ground in its own right. A stone wall snaps onto the shared edge, standing upright, almost as stable as the foundations and still blue. When the builder lays a timber ceiling across from the wall, the preview turns green, then yellow as they reach a second span out over open air. A stone pillar set into the ground at the yellow edge carries that outer span and turns it green again. The builder has learned in a minute where the structure is strong and where a raider will strike. The numbers behind this example are in [Building System (Technical)](<Building-system (Technical).md#12-design-validation-cases>).

## Weather and Environment

Buildings must suit their biome or suffer for it. A design that works in the dry [Plains](Biomes/Plains.md) may rot in the rain-heavy [Jungle](Biomes/Jungle.md), sag under mountain snow, or flood in a swamp, so material and location are part of settlement strategy rather than decoration. Weathered pieces lose strength over time until they are repaired, and a material that suits its country ages slowly.

Buildings show their age and their weather. Every piece remembers when it was placed and in what country it stands, and it ages on its own surface as the [Building Materials](Art-direction/Building-materials.md) briefs describe: fresh timber silvers within seasons, iron weeps rust down the stone below it, moss creeps into the joints on the wet side, and limewash dulls to cream. A base that has been kept looks kept, and one left to decay looks it long before it falls. Weather lies on top of that: snow settles on roofs and ledges and slides from steep pitches, stone darkens and runs wet in rain, and soot blackens the wall above a hearth. No two placed pieces are quite alike either. Each takes a small variation of its own when it is placed, a shift of colour, a different spread of wear, one of a few shapes of the same part, so a palisade a hundred posts long never repeats itself along its length.

| Biome | Challenge | Adaptive Solution |
|---|---|---|
| [Tundra](Biomes/Tundra.md) | Cold, ice, snow loads | Insulated structures, heated foundations |
| [Scorching Desert](Biomes/Scorching-desert.md) | Heat, sandstorms | Clay and brick, reflective surfaces, hardened walls |
| [Jungle](Biomes/Jungle.md) | Rain, rot, dense foliage | Elevated platforms, treated timber |
| [Mountain Range](Biomes/Mountain-range.md) | Avalanches, exposed cliffs | Reinforced anchoring, wind-resistant design |
| [Swamp](Biomes/Swamp.md) | Flooding, decay | Raised floor systems, waterproof materials |

## Damage and Siege

Anything that stands can be brought down, and almost anything can do it, at very different speeds. Damage comes in kinds, and each material resists them differently:

- **Cutting** from axes and blades bites quickly into thatch and timber and barely scratches stone.
- **Crushing** from picks, hammers, battering rams, catapults, and the weight of great creatures is what stone fears, and it is the steady work of any siege.
- **Fire** from torches, burning oil, fire magic, and the breath of a [Royal Dragon](Creatures/Royal-dragon.md) devours thatch and timber and only scorches stone. Burning pieces keep losing strength until the fire is put out or rain douses it, and flames spread from one timber piece to the next.
- **Explosive** force from the largest fire spells, such as [Fireball](Magic/Spells/Fire/Fireball.md), damages every material, and it is the closest thing this world has to a breaching charge.
- **Earth** magic shakes and cracks stone and the ground under foundations, turning a mage into a sapper; [Fissure](Magic/Spells/Earth/Fissure.md) is the school's siege spell.

Because every opening inside a claim is shut to a raider, studying a wall is a matter of deciding what to break, not what to open: a shuttered window is a thinner wall, a barred door a heavier one, and a gate is the piece a ram was made for. A lone raider with a pickaxe can start hacking at a stone wall, and given enough uninterrupted hours they will get through it, but every swing is loud and every hour is an hour for defenders to arrive. The better answers cost more to bring and are harder to hide: rams and siege engines must be built on site or hauled in, war beasts must be raised and fed, and a mage who can breach stone has spent years learning to. Walls do not have to be broken at all if they can be climbed. Siege ladders and grappling lines go over a wall, and flyers come down inside it if nothing stops them.

A wall is equally strong from either side, so what protects a base is how it is laid out, not which way its walls face. Nothing built can be climbed with bare hands, as [Movement](Movement.md#climbing) sets out, so getting over a wall means bringing a ladder or a grapnel and being seen using it. Defence is a matter of design more than thickness, and the strongest bases are hard to reach, hard to read, and slow to break into. Roofs over courtyards deny flyers a landing, spiked wall-tops slow climbers, barred and shuttered windows keep a stone wall from being only as strong as its glass, stone cladding keeps fire off a timber core, archer posts with a clear view of the sky cover the approaches from above, and a winding entrance turns a battering ram into a slow problem. Every piece has a set amount of health, damage lowers it, and only repair restores it. A builder with a hammer and the piece's materials restores a set amount of that health with each repair action, and repair works in the middle of a fight. The one limit is that a piece cannot be mended while it is itself being struck; every other piece of the base can be. Defenders can therefore patch the far wall while the gate is under the ram, and a raid is never won by a clock, only by health, layout and defenders: a strong, well laid out base outlasts a long assault and a weak one falls quickly. Offline raiding stays fully open, with no raid windows, no offline protection and no timers of any kind. Upkeep does not repair raid damage either; it only keeps weathering at bay, so every wound a raid leaves needs the hammer.

Luring a creature onto a base is a legitimate and expensive way to attack it. A creature's body and blows do crushing damage to pieces by the same impact rules as everything else in [Melee Combat](Melee-combat.md#creatures), in proportion to its weight, so a [Stone Giant](Creatures/Stone-giant.md) fed crystals along a trail, a hardened [Slime](Creatures/Slime.md) led by bait, or a [Titan Turtle](Creatures/Titan-turtle.md) hauled round by its chains is a siege engine that no one built. The creature still follows its own drives and never an order, so the lure, whether bait, a crystal trail or a steered course, has to be kept up by attackers who are present, and when it runs out, or the creature is hurt enough to give up as its own file describes, it leaves. A base answers it like any other raid, by its layout, its guards and its bonded creatures, and an owner who is absent has no special protection.

How raids unfold, who may raid whom, and what happens to a base whose owners are asleep are set out in [Raids and Conquest](Raids.md).

## Claims, Upkeep, and Decay

Land is claimed with a claim marker, a crafted piece placed on a base's foundations. It projects building rights outward from every foundation connected to it, so the claimed ground grows and shrinks with the base itself. Inside a claim only its owner, and those they or their guild grant access under [Guilds and Factions](Guilds-and-factions.md), can build, and only they can open its doors, gates, hatches, shutters, and containers; to everyone else each of these is a closed thing to break or to go around, as [Fittings](#fittings) sets out. Outsiders can place only temporary siege pieces, such as ladders, grappling lines, rams, and siege engines, which decay quickly. Claims cannot overlap and must keep a gap between them, so no one can wall in a rival's base with their own foundations.

A claim is kept by upkeep, not by attendance. The claim marker holds a store of the materials the base is built from, and it draws on that store each day to keep every connected piece in repair. The daily cost grows with the number of structural pieces, and grows faster for very large bases, so a sprawling fortress is a standing expense that only an organised group can feed. Only structural pieces pay: fittings, chests, beds, troughs, and furniture count toward a claim's piece ceiling but cost nothing to keep. While the store lasts, no one has to log in. A group can leave for a long campaign, or a solo player can step away for a week, and the walls stand. The store holds several materials, and each is drawn down for the pieces made of it. When one runs out, only those pieces start to fail, so a base short of iron bands sees its reinforced work weaken while its timber still stands. Decay sets in from the outside inward: exposed outer pieces whose upkeep has run dry weaken first, weathering faster the weaker their material, and when every material has been missing for long enough the claim lapses. A lapsed base is abandoned. Anyone may demolish it and salvage its materials, and the land returns to the world for someone else to build on.

Holding more than one claim costs more than the sum of its parts. Each additional claim an owner keeps, whether a character or a guild, pays a higher daily rate than the one before it, on top of what its own size demands. A guild that sprawls across the frontier therefore bleeds faster with every outpost it adds and must shed the land it cannot defend or feed, which is what keeps the frontier open to newcomers and keeps old holdings from hardening into permanent ones. Claims nobody feeds decay and lapse as above.

The marker is also a target. Destroying it ends the claim's protection at once, which lets attackers build inside the walls and starts the base decaying, so the marker is usually hidden at the heart of a base behind its strongest walls. The base is then up for the taking: the first new claim marker set on its foundations becomes the claim, whoever places it, the old owner included, so a raid that breaks the marker is a race to replace it. Foundations and pillars that are not connected to any claim decay within days, which keeps the land from being fenced off with scattered pieces. Every claim also has a ceiling on the number of pieces it can hold, so that no single base can overwhelm the server running its region. A fitting counts as a third of a piece against it, a default each server may change, so a builder who glazes, shutters, and trims a house spends far less of the ceiling than the house's walls did, and dressing a base well is never a choice against building it large. A base that straddles the line between two regions is held whole by the region that holds its marker, so a claim never splits in two.

## Outskirts Plots

A player who wants a home without a war has one safe answer: a plot in the guarded outskirts of a [great city](Structures/Great-cities.md#outskirts-plots). These are small plots inside the patrolled ring around the city, leased from it for a regular tax rather than held by upkeep and a claim marker. A plot takes a modest house, a workshop, and a garden, under a low piece ceiling, and everything on it is beyond any raider's reach: the plot cannot be raided, its pieces cannot be damaged by anyone but its owner, and a character that logs out there is as safe as in the city itself, as [Death and Loss](Death-and-loss.md#logging-out) sets out. An unpaid tax lets the plot decay and lapse like any other claim.

The safety is paid for in what the plot lacks. There are no resource nodes inside the ring, no room for more than a small household's needs, and no frontier to hold, so a builder who lives in the outskirts buys their stone, timber, and ore from the frontier's hands rather than working it themselves. The player who wants the frontier's resources and space takes its risks, including being raided while offline, which stays fully open beyond the ring, as [Raids and Conquest](Raids.md#player-base-raids) describes.

## Shelter for Creatures

There is no single stable building. A tamed creature counts as sheltered wherever the pieces around it meet its needs, and the same rules hold for a wolf in a lean-to and a dragon in a stone hall. Four conditions decide it:

- **Food in reach**: a stocked feeding trough close enough for the creature to eat from without wandering. A trough feeds every creature within its short reach and empties faster the more mouths it serves.
- **A roof overhead**: the creature stands under cover, judged from the roofs above it rather than from walls around it.
- **The right climate**: warmth for creatures that suffer in the cold, from a hearth or brazier in the same space, and shade and cool air for creatures that suffer in heat, from a roof and from a cooling source, a placeable such as a cistern of cold water that mirrors the hearth for heat-sensitive creatures and works over the same short reach and unbroken space. Which a creature needs is part of its nature.
- **Room for its body**: enough space to stand, turn, and lie down. A wolf fits a lean-to, a buffalo needs a barn, and a dragon needs a hall.

A sheltered creature holds a trust or parental bond exactly as it was for as long as its trough stays stocked, which is what lets a player leave for a week without losing a mount, as set out in [Taming and Control](Taming-and-control.md#bonds-while-away). Shelter does nothing for a slave bond, whose pressure drains wherever the creature stands. A coerced creature is kept by restraint instead: a tether post with its chain, or a barred pen, both placed like any other piece of a shelter, hold a creature where it is when its pressure has run out, as [Slave Bonding](Taming-and-control.md#slave-bonding) describes. A restraint holds a body and nothing more, and a restrained creature still needs its trough. A shelter keeps a creature in good condition, and wounds heal fastest there. Flyers follow exactly the same rules; how a rider gets their flyer out and into the air is the rider's own affair. A shelter inside a claim falls with the base, and what raiders can do to the creatures inside is set out in [Taming and Control](Taming-and-control.md#bonds-while-away).

## Bases on Creatures

Two creatures carry bases on their backs. A [Titan Turtle](Creatures/Titan-turtle.md) bears a saddle structure strapped to its shell, and a [Flying Leviathan](Creatures/Flying-leviathan.md) carries a deck built on foundations fixed along its back. Both use the same pieces and the same snapping as building on land, starting from a special foundation that can be fixed only to the creature, and both have a piece budget that keeps the base readable and the creature able to move. Everything built on them counts against what they can carry: a laden turtle grows slower and harder to steer, and every tonne on a leviathan's deck draws on its mana, as described in [Flight](Flight.md#mana-borne-flight). The two differ in who answers for the base. A leviathan's deck needs no claim marker: its keeper holds the building rights, and the creature's own care takes the place of upkeep. A turtle's base follows the ordinary rules for any structure, whether the turtle is bonded or wild. It needs a claim marker, pays upkeep, holds to a piece ceiling set by the turtle's size and strength, and can be raided while its owners are away like any base on land. The claim covers the shell only and travels with the turtle, so it never claims ground the turtle walks over, and the bond grants no building rights of its own: whoever holds the marker holds the base, and a keeper who has tamed the turtle holds nothing on its back unless they have claimed it. A turtle needs no shelter at all, so the shell is a home that asks nothing of its bearer.

## Building in Practice

The system scales with the people using it. A solo player keeps a compact outpost, a hidden cabin, or a crafting shelter cheap enough to stock for weeks. A small group raises a fortified camp, a resource depot, or a frontier workshop. A guild builds halls, fortified towns, marketplaces, and layered keeps that become landmarks, conflict targets, and a standing commitment of upkeep. At every scale the same questions apply: what the walls are made of, what holds them up, how the materials got there, and who keeps the marker fed.

Size also decides how a base is defended. A small group cannot hold a large base, and does not need one: a small, well-hidden base is cheap to keep and hard to find, and its owners stand guard through hired or enslaved NPC guards, whose rules are set out in [Non-Player Characters](NPCs.md), and through bonded creatures that defend the ground they are sheltered on. A guild needs far more room for its people, stores, and workshops, and a large base has more wall, more doors, and more sky to cover, which is more work to defend however many hands it has. The contested ground and the conquest that go with it are the large guilds' game, as [Guilds and Factions](Guilds-and-factions.md#territory-and-conquest) explains.
