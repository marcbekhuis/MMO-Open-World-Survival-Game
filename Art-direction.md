# Art Direction

The game's look is photorealistic, and its identity is a young, living world in which everything that grows, flows, or breathes is quickened by mana. This document states that visual thesis, the shape language and material discipline that carry it, the things the world is never, the way magic sits in the same frame as mud and rope, and the signatures that keep the regions apart from one another, so that an artist, an image generator, or a stranger looking at a single screenshot can tell what world they are in and where.

## The Visual Thesis

The world is a mythic age in its first flush, not the ruin of a fallen one. As [Setting and Lore](Setting-and-lore.md) establishes, magic wells from the world itself, through leylines and primal currents, so the land, the weather, and the beasts are all alive with it, and the people moving across that land are small, hard-handed newcomers working it with timber, rope, stone, and iron. The look follows from that picture. Nature is the large, old, confident presence in every frame: bigger than the player, more complex than anything built, and faintly aware. People are the brief, human-scaled presence: practical, patched, and recent. Beauty and dread come from the same source, a world that is vividly alive, and the difference between a place that enchants and a place that threatens is what that life is doing, not how much of it there is.

Contrast, the swing from the most beautiful country in any game to scenes out of real horror, is the mood tool that serves this identity and not the identity itself. A plain and a swamp are two moods of one living world, held together by the thesis, the shape language, and the materials below, and the journey between them is an arc a player feels because the world around them is recognisably the same hand's work throughout.

## Photorealistic Foundation

Fidelity targets photorealism on Unreal Engine 5. [Nanite](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/nanite-virtualized-geometry-in-unreal-engine) virtualised geometry carries film-quality detail without hand-built levels of detail, [Lumen](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/lumen-global-illumination-and-reflections-in-unreal-engine) provides dynamic global illumination and reflections so light behaves believably as the day and weather change, and [Virtual Shadow Maps](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/virtual-shadow-maps-in-unreal-engine) deliver high-resolution shadows that hold up across a large, dynamically lit world. Materials obey real-world scale and physically based standards, characters are built to a high human fidelity, and surfaces wear the dirt, damage, and age that a survival world demands. And because the world is only ever seen in first person, that fidelity sits directly in front of the player at eye level, where its detail and scale carry their full weight.

Photorealism across a seamless persistent world is expensive, and the art is authored with that cost in mind rather than against it. Assets are built for streaming and for the budgets defined in [Dynamic Culling & Render Distance](Dynamic-culling-and-render-distance.md) and realised through the World Partition and rendering pipeline in [Server Architecture (Technical)](<Server-architecture (Technical).md>). Fidelity and performance are balanced there, so the look described here always reads against what the engine can sustain at scale.

## Shape Language and Material Discipline

Two grammars share every frame and are kept visibly apart. The wild is built of curves, taper, and asymmetry: trunks that lean and flare, rock that cleaves along its own grain, rivers that cut their own banks, creatures whose silhouettes are legible at a distance because each one is one strong shape with one or two exaggerated features. Anything people make is built of the simplest geometry that bears its load: straight runs, square-hewn beams, lashed and pegged joints, stacked and mortared stone, with the tool marks, patches, and repairs left in sight. A palisade is a line of felled trunks, a bridge is a span of planks over two stout posts, and neither pretends to be anything else. Where the two meet, the wild wins by default: moss takes the north face of a wall, roots lift paving, and a road shows the ruts of the carts that use it.

Each people carries a material palette of its own, and it is kept narrow so that a rooftop or a doorway names the builder at a glance. Human work is timber, wattle and daub, fired clay, iron, and wool, crowded and repaired. Elven work is living and shaped wood, bark, woven fibre, and bone-pale stone, woven from living stems that stay in leaf wherever they stand, and at its finest built against a great tree. Dwarven work is dressed stone, bronze, steel, and soot, massive and square, cut to last. These palettes are drawn from the architecture in [Races](Races.md) and the settlement files it links, and they are worn, weathered, and dirty to the same degree everywhere: nothing in the world is new, clean, and untouched except what a character has just made, and even that is marked by the hands that made it.

This discipline is applied material by material in the [Building Materials](Art-direction/Building-materials.md) briefs, which say what thatch, planked timber, cobblestone, or warded stone looks like at eye level and what each is never to look like. Placement behaviour that the building system shares with other survival builders implies none of their look: a snapping grid and a tiered ladder of pieces are mechanics, and the surfaces on them are drawn from these briefs, not from any other game's kit.

Colour is spent with discipline. The base of any scene is earth, stone, bark, cloth, and leaf in muted, physically plausible values, and saturated colour is reserved for what carries meaning: living flowers, the plumage of birds, a banner that names an allegiance, the warm lamp in a window, blood, and mana. A player's eye should land on something because it matters, and the rest of the frame should be rich in detail and quiet in colour.

## Mana in the Frame

Mana is a physical force in this world, and it is drawn as one: it acts on material things and leaves material traces, and it is lit by the same sun and the same fire as everything else. It is never a screen overlay, never a neon glow, and never clean. In the open world it shows itself in the quiet signs a land under pressure from a leyline would show, a shimmer in the air over a spring that never freezes, trees that lean toward a current, lichen and spores that give off a faint light in deep shade, frost-white growth on stone in summer, rivers that run a colour no river should. The currents do not all look alike. The high, bright currents that lift the floating islands are warm, pale, and rising, the colour of morning haze and white-gold; the deep currents beneath the mountains are cold, dark, and sinking, blue-black and slow. A viewer who knows only this rule can read which way a place leans.

Magic cast by a person follows the same rule. A spell is an event in the physical scene: it throws real light that casts shadows and fades, it scorches turf, shatters ice, tears earth, and leaves soot, ash, steam, and cracked stone behind it, and the hands that cast it are as muddy as the hands that hold a rope. A mage's robe is wool with burn marks, and an enchanted blade is a worn blade with one restrained tell, a thin line of cold light along the fuller or a faint hum of heat haze above the edge, never a full glow. Nothing a player owns glows brighter than a campfire, and that ceiling is crossed only by the great events of the world, the awakening of an elder power or the opening of a dungeon's heart, where the excess is itself the point. Magic and mud belong in the same frame, in the same light, with the same dirt on them.

## What This World Is Never

The world is never a ruin of a fallen empire. Where something ancient stands, it reads as grown by the world or worn by it, half-taken by roots, rain, and moss, and not as the broken columns of a vanished civilisation; the age is young and the land is still making itself. It is never neon, never a light show, and never a place where magic is drawn as clean, glowing, floating script. It is never storybook or cartoon: no soft rounded proportions, no candy saturation, no painterly gloss laid over the photography, and no heroic cinematic posing of a character who is only ever seen through their own eyes. It is never pristine: no spotless plate armour, no unweathered stone, no gleaming town. It is never industrial, since technology stops short of gunpowder, and it never borrows the clockwork and brass of steam fantasy. It is never costume before function: clothing and armour are drawn as what a body in this country would wear to survive it. And it is never gore as spectacle; the horror is in dread and consequence, as [Violence Made Real](#violence-made-real) sets out.

## Contrast as Mood

Within that single identity, regions commit hard to a register instead of blending toward a safe middle. A sunlit plain or a field in flower can be among the most beautiful, colourful, and peaceful spaces in any game, while a [Swamp](Biomes/Swamp.md) or a mythic-dangerous region can feel genuinely wrong, gloomy, and oppressive, the kind of place a player does not want to enter alone at night. The power lives in the whiplash between them: leaving warmth and light behind to push into dread, and the relief of breaking back out into open beauty, turns travel itself into an emotional arc, so the [Plains](Biomes/Plains.md) and the swamp read as opposite poles of the same world.

## Light, Colour, and Atmosphere per Region

Every biome carries its own palette, key light, atmosphere, and colour grade, chosen to commit to one emotion and described as a pair of poles for each region: where its colour sits between warm and cold and between saturated and drained, and where its key light comes from and how hard it falls. Peaceful regions run warm and saturated under soft, golden light, lush and inviting and open. Horror regions desaturate toward sickly or cold casts, sink into heavy fog and deep shadow, and lean on unsettling colour, the bruised greens and bone-and-blood notes that tell a player something here is hostile to them. Lumen, Virtual Shadow Maps, and volumetric atmosphere do most of this work, tuned per region through post-process volumes. The contrast is not only mood; it is information, because the look of a place telegraphs its danger before a single creature appears.

### The Four Woodlands

Four biomes are green woodland, and green is the one colour they share. Each is therefore given a signature of its own across five things: how high and how dense the canopy is, the palette, the key light, the silhouette of the plant that defines it, and what a survivor reads there to stay alive. A concept plate, a screenshot, or a generated image of one must not be mistakable for another, and the test is simple: with the creatures and the interface removed, a viewer should still name the biome from the trees alone.

**Forest.** The [Forest](Biomes/Forest.md) is the benign green, a temperate mixed woodland with its canopy at about twenty-five to thirty metres, open enough that patches of sky show through and sightlines run sixty metres or more along corridors, clearings, and ridgelines. The palette is warm mid-greens broken by ochre, rust, and the grey of bark, and it turns with the seasons more than any other woodland. The key light is low, golden, and dappled, falling in broken shafts through the crowns. Its keystone silhouette is the broad, rounded crown of oak and beech standing over narrow conifer spires, tiered in a clear layer of canopy, shrub, and floor. A survivor reads it by ear and by ground: birdsong that stops, tracks in the leaf litter, cairns and ridgelines that fix a position. It is the one woodland that explains itself.

**Ancient Forest.** The [Ancient Forest](Biomes/Ancient-forest.md) is the cathedral green. Its giants rise hundreds of metres and lock into a roof so high and dense that the floor lives in blue-green twilight, with sightlines long between the trunks but almost no horizon, because the canopy closes the sky. The palette is deep blue-green, silver bark, wet moss, and near-black shadow, with the water of its rivers clear enough to show every stone. The key light is rare and vertical, a few narrow shafts of white-gold falling a hundred metres to the moss, with cool ambient fill and a faint cold glow in the deepest hollows from its mana-fed lichen. The keystone silhouette is the pillar and the arch: colossal buttressed trunks with root flare wider than a house, and roots arching over rivers as bridges. A survivor reads it by restraint and by warnings: the wrongness of a disturbed trunk, the stillness before a [Treant](Creatures/Treant.md) stirs, the canopy settlement of the [Elves](Races/Elf.md) above, and the knowledge that harm done to the wood is answered.

**Jungle.** The [Jungle](Biomes/Jungle.md) is the crowded green, built in strata: emergent crowns at forty to fifty metres, a closed canopy beneath them, a dense understory five to fifteen metres up, and a floor where a player rarely sees more than fifteen metres in any direction. The palette is saturated, wet, and heavy, deep greens and black-green shade struck by hot accents, the red, orange, and violet of flowers, pigment plants, and bird plumage. The key light is high and hard at midday, then lost in steam, so the lit parts of the frame are overexposed and the shaded parts nearly black, with wet highlights on every leaf and volumetric mist hanging in the lower levels. The keystone silhouette is the tangle: buttress-rooted emergent trees draped in curtains of liana, broad drooping leaves, and, rising over it all, the stepped mass of the great overgrown temple. A survivor reads it by the chorus: a flock lifting over the canopy, the [Four-Armed Monkeys](Creatures/Four-armed-monkey.md) falling silent, flooded paths and river sound.

**Bamboo Jungle.** The [Bamboo Jungle](Biomes/Bamboo-jungle.md) is the striped green. Its canopy is a ceiling of feathery tops twenty to thirty metres up, and below it the world is a field of vertical rods standing a metre or two apart, so sightlines are short, five to ten metres, and shift when the wind moves the stalks. The palette is pale and washed, straw-gold stalks, yellow-green leaf, and silver-green shadow, with the lowest saturation and the lowest contrast of the four. The key light is backlit and diffuse, coming through the translucent stalks as vertical bars of light and shadow that slide with the wind. The keystone silhouette is the comb: regular, ringed, cylindrical rods repeating to the edge of sight, topped with soft plumes, with stilted villages and lashed walkways raised among them. A survivor reads it by stillness and by wrongness in the pattern: a grove that should sway and does not, a stalk that is the wrong thickness, the sudden loss of a sightline.

The four are separated by the same five dials turned to different values: canopy from open and mid-height to a sealed vault, a layered roof, or a thin ceiling over a stalk field; palette from warm and seasonal to cold and deep, hot and saturated, or pale and washed; light from golden and dappled to rare shafts, hard noon and steam, or striped backlight; silhouette from the round crown to the pillar, the tangle, and the comb; and the survival read from sound and ground to restraint, chorus, and stillness.

## Violence Made Real

Because rendering is photoreal and the game holds to the standard set in [Content and Tone](Content-and-tone.md), violence carries real visual weight. Wounds, blood, and dismemberment are rendered convincingly, and the darkest regions lean into body horror as a deliberate aesthetic rather than a constant one. The aim is dread and consequence, not gratuitous spectacle, and the gore-intensity control in [Player Settings](Player-settings.md) lets each player scale how much of it reaches their own screen without changing what is happening in the world.

## Characters and Creatures

The three playable races described in [Player Progression](Player.md) are rendered to a high human fidelity, with believable skin, hair, armour, cloth, and the grime and wear of a hard life, and character creation is supported visually so players can see themselves in the world. Creatures share that fidelity, from the small wildlife that gives a biome life to the great mythic beasts of the [bestiary](Creatures.md), each built on one strong readable silhouette in the shape language above. The elder powers in particular are meant to look awe-inspiring or terrifying as their role demands, so that meeting a [Royal Dragon](Creatures/Royal-dragon.md) or a [Kraken](Creatures/Kraken.md) lands as an event the moment it comes into view.

## Coherence at Scale

A shared style guide keeps photorealism and extreme regional contrast from pulling the world apart. The thesis, shape language, material palettes, and rules for mana above are its fixed core; common physically based rendering standards, consistent lighting rules, controlled per-biome palettes, and shared conventions for scale and asset construction sit on top of them, so that however far apart two regions sit in mood, they still feel built by the same hand. The texture maps, texel density, repetition limits, and arm's-length acceptance check for every surface are fixed in [Surface Authoring](Art-direction/Surface-authoring.md). Transitions between neighbouring biomes are blended with care so the contrast stays dramatic without jarring pop as a player crosses the seam between one register and the next.

## Authoring an Asset

An artist or generator starting an asset reads, in order, these art rules, the design document for the system the asset belongs to together with its technical guide, the material brief in [Building Materials](Art-direction/Building-materials.md) or the cultural brief for the people who made it, and the source-art contract that the asset generators work to. The brief for a piece of work declares its piece list, the visual variants each piece needs, the review references it will be judged against, and its delivery level, which is one of three: source models, textured engine assets, or playable registered pieces with snapping, collision, and interaction in place. Nothing is assumed to be delivered beyond the stated level. Each hand-off records the assumptions it could not resolve, links the review images, and links the validation results, so the next reader sees what was checked and what was taken on trust.
