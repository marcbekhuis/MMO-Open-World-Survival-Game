# Flight

Flight comes in two forms. Gliding is open to anyone who crafts a wing-rig, and it can only descend. True flight on the back of a flying creature, whether it beats its way aloft on wings or is held up by mana, is one of the hardest-won freedoms in the game. Both answer to the rule set out in [Travel and Mounts](Travel-and-mounts.md): a faster way to travel must add value to distance, not erase it. A flyer crosses country that would take days on foot, but it tires and must land, it carries little, and the sky has weather of its own. This document covers how gliding and mounted flight work, what they cost, and how they change the way a player sees the world. Everything in [Creature Riding](Creature-riding.md) applies to a flying mount as well; what follows is what the air adds.

## Gliding

A wing-rig is a folding frame of light wood or bone with hide or treated silk stretched across it. It is strapped to the back and opened with both arms. It lifts no one. A glider needs height to launch, from a cliff edge, a tower, a ridge, or the rim of a [floating island](Biomes/Floating-islands.md), and from there it trades height for distance in a long, controlled descent. Rising air slows the descent: an updraft along a sunlit cliff or the wind driving up a ridge lets a skilled glider stretch a flight much further. Rising air never carries a glider higher than the point it launched from, and every flight ends lower than it began.

The rig's limits are physical. A glider flies in first person with the arms holding the frame, so the hands are full and no weapon or spell can be used aloft. Weight drags it down, and a heavily laden character sinks fast, while an overloaded one cannot glide at all, which keeps the rig a tool for a traveller's own body and kit rather than for cargo. Pulling up too hard stalls it, landing too fast injures, and trees, water, and rough ground make every landing a choice. Rain soaks the skin and makes it sluggish, a storm makes gliding suicidal, and the frame wears and tears like any other gear from [Crafting](Crafting.md).

Within those limits the glider changes how height is used. A climber who reaches a summit in the [Mountain Range](Biomes/Mountain-range.md) can come down in minutes rather than hours, a scout can sweep a valley from a ridge and land beyond it, and a raider caught on a clifftop has a way off it. The floating islands, hard to climb up to, become much easier to leave. A glider is also plain to see, a dark wing against the sky, so a descent shows every watcher below where the glider will land.

## Flying Mounts

A flying mount is any creature of the [bestiary](Creatures.md) strong enough to carry a rider aloft, and every one of them is hard to win. The [Flying Leviathan](Creatures/Flying-leviathan.md) must be raised from a calf on the floating islands before it can carry its keeper anywhere, and the [Royal Dragon](Creatures/Royal-dragon.md) accepts a rider only as something closer to an ally than a possession. Taming is the gate in every case, through the methods of [Taming and Control](Taming-and-control.md), so flight is a goal a player works toward over a long stretch of play.

A flyer is still a creature with a will. It refuses orders that would kill it, it carries fear and fatigue, and its bond decides how far it will follow its rider into danger, exactly as on the ground. A flyer is ridden in a flight saddle with a harness that holds a conscious, braced rider through banks and dives. A rider is not thrown by ordinary manoeuvring, and one who wants to leave the saddle in the air has to unbuckle first, which is a deliberate act that makes sense only with a wing-rig on their back.

Flyers stay aloft in one of two ways. Winged flyers such as the [Royal Dragon](Creatures/Royal-dragon.md) work muscle against air, and the sections from launch to weather below describe how they fly and what it costs them. The [Flying Leviathan](Creatures/Flying-leviathan.md) is held up by mana instead and follows the separate rules of [Mana-Borne Flight](#mana-borne-flight).

## Taking Off and Landing

Getting into the air is the hardest part of any flight, and flyers do not all manage it the same way. How a species launches follows from its body, the power of its wings against the weight they have to lift, and its bestiary entry says which way it flies.

- **Standing launch.** A flyer whose wings are strong enough for its weight can spring from a standstill and beat its way straight up. The [Royal Dragon](Creatures/Royal-dragon.md) launches this way, rearing back on its hind legs and driving its wings down in a blast of grit. A standing launch needs little room beyond space overhead for the wings to clear. It is also the most strenuous way to fly, loud, plain to see, and expensive enough that a tired flyer may not manage it at all.
- **Run-up launch.** A heavier or longer-winged flyer cannot lift itself from a standstill. It has to run along firm, level, open ground, wings half-spread and beating, until its speed carries it off the earth, the way a swan runs across water. It needs a clear strip, so it cannot launch from forest, broken rock, deep snow, a bog, or a crowded camp. For the few seconds of the run the flyer is committed, neither able to stop easily nor yet out of reach.
- **Drop launch.** Any flyer can launch by dropping from a height, a cliff edge, a ridge, a tower, or a ledge, and letting the fall give it the speed its wings need. It costs almost nothing, which makes high perches the cheapest place to begin a flight and the natural roosts of the heavier flyers.

Load and wind change every launch. Each kilogram aboard lengthens a run-up and makes a standing launch harder, until a heavily laden standing flyer needs a few running strides or a drop of its own, and an overloaded one will not try at all. Launching into the wind shortens the run, because the air is already moving over the wings, so a run-up flyer turns to face the wind before it starts; with the wind behind it, the same strip can prove too short. In the saddle a run-up reads like a gallop that suddenly goes smooth. The pounding stops, the ground falls away, and the wingbeats take over.

Landing follows the same body. A standing flyer can settle almost straight down into a small space, while a run-up flyer comes in fast and has to run off its speed along open ground, and every flyer lands more gently into the wind. A flyer can land somewhere it cannot take off from again. A run-up flyer that comes down in a small clearing has to walk out to open ground before it can fly, and one that lands at the foot of a cliff has lost the drop it might have launched from. The rider chooses where to come down and the mount chooses the exact spot, refusing ground that looks soft, cramped, or watched by something it fears, and a run-up flyer also refuses ground it could not leave. The same holds at home. A flyer is sheltered like any tamed creature under the [Building System](Building-system.md#shelter-for-creatures), and its shelter has to open onto the launch it needs: open sky for a standing flyer, a ledge for a drop launcher, a clear strip for a run-up flyer.

Landing is the most exposed moment in flight. A flyer on the ground is a large, tired animal that cannot easily get away, and its rider is a lone traveller in country they chose from the air. That is why the places where flyers come down matter so much on a long journey, as the section on endurance below shows.

## Flying in First Person

Mounted flight uses the same reins and intent as riding on the ground, extended into three dimensions. The rider sets the heading, whether to climb or descend, and the pace, from a slow glide through a steady cruise to a hard sprint, and can tip the mount over into a dive. The mount does the flying: it handles the wingbeats, balance, and turbulence, and it pulls up on its own short of a cliff face. From the saddle the rider reads the animal the way a horseman does, through the rhythm of the wingbeats, the working of the shoulders under the saddle, and the breathing. When the beats grow laboured and the mount begins to sink between strokes, it is tiring.

Flying from the rider's own eyes is the most intense view in the game, and it is the main reason for the motion comfort options set out in [Accessibility](Accessibility.md).

## Diving

Height is stored effort. A flyer spends stamina to climb, and a dive gives that height back as speed. With its wings folded or swept, a diving flyer accelerates faster than anything in the sky can fly level, at almost no cost to itself, and at the bottom it can pull out and carry much of that speed forward or zoom back up through part of the height it lost. Riders use the dive to cover a short distance in a hurry, to shake off a pursuer that cannot follow it down, and above all to strike. A flyer coming out of a dive hits a target on the ground or in the air with the force of a lance charge, as [Creature Riding](Creature-riding.md#fighting-from-the-saddle) describes, and then has to climb away and regain height before it can do so again.

Every dive ends in a pull-out, and the pull-out is where dives go wrong. The faster the dive, the more height the flyer needs to level off, and a heavy load stretches that distance further. The mount judges the pull-out with its own instincts and begins it on its own once it senses the ground coming too fast. A weakly bonded flyer takes the pull-out from its rider early, spending its speed well above the target. A well-bonded one trusts its rider and holds the dive lower and longer, which is what makes a diving strike land. No flyer holds a dive into the ground, the trees, or the water, so a rider can make a dive daring but never suicidal. A dive in a crosswind or through gusting air near cliffs drifts off its line and needs more room to recover.

A dive does not have to end in a strike or a climb. Curved out correctly, it ends in a glide, and that is how experienced riders cover distance cheaply. A well-shaped pull-out brings the flyer level with its wings spread wide and its speed intact, and it carries that speed into a long, flat glide that costs almost no stamina: the wings hold still, the mount's breathing settles, and the ground runs past beneath. The glide slowly spends the speed and then the height, so in time the flyer has to beat or climb again. Even so, climbing on rising air, diving, and gliding out, then doing it again, carries a flyer across country for far less than beating level the whole way.

Everything depends on the curve. A pull-out that is too sharp brakes the flyer against the air, bleeding off the speed it gathered and straining its wings, so it comes out slow and has to work its way back up to a glide. One begun too late reaches the bottom too low, with no height left to glide from, and one begun too early turns the dive's speed back into height it already had. The right curve changes with how fast the dive has become, the load aboard, and the wind, and judging it is something riders learn over many flights. A mount left to save itself pulls out by instinct, and that rescue is always the hard, sharp kind: it keeps the flyer alive and throws the glide away. In the saddle a good transition is felt more than seen. The roar of the dive eases into a steady hiss, the pressure of the harness lifts, and the wings lock out with no flutter along their edges.

A dive is plain to anyone below. The air roars over folded wings, the flyer's shadow grows quickly across the ground, and for those last seconds the flyer is committed to a line. A target that hears it coming can step aside, put a tree or a rock overhang between itself and the sky, or brace a spear against the angle of approach, and a flyer that misses has spent its height and is now slow and low.

## Flying with and against the Wind

Wind decides what a flight costs. Flying into a headwind, a flyer works harder for every kilometre and covers less of them: its wings beat as fast as ever, but the ground below crawls past and its stamina drains quickly. With the wind behind it, the same flyer is carried along, covering ground quickly at an easy cruise and spending far less to get there. A crosswind pushes a flyer off its line. The mount angles into it to hold its course, which costs a little more than still air and makes it drift whenever the rider stops correcting. The wing-rig follows the same rule: a glide with the wind behind it reaches much further than one into the wind.

Wind is not the same at every height. Near the ground it is broken and gusty, torn up by trees, ridges, and buildings. Higher up it blows stronger and steadier, so a rider facing a headwind may find an easier layer by descending into the sheltered air near the ground, or a stronger tailwind by climbing into the cold. A rider reads the wind before committing to a route, from the drift of clouds, the lean of smoke from a settlement, waves running through grass, whitecaps on open water, and the banners on a town wall. The prevailing winds of each region and season, described in [Weather, Time, and Seasons](Weather-time-and-seasons.md), make flying routes lopsided. A crossing that is quick and cheap with the wind can be a long, exhausting flight home against it, and a rider who spends their flyer's endurance on the way out may find there is not enough left to return. Planning a flight means planning the way back, with landing places sited for the return leg as well as the outward one.

## Endurance and the Chain of Landings

Flight costs a creature far more than travel on the ground. Climbing is the most expensive thing a flyer can do, level flight is steady work and heavier against the wind, a dive spends height rather than stamina, a glide drawn out of a well-curved dive is the cheapest way to cover ground, and only rising air gives a tired flyer back some of what it spent. Reading the sky is therefore a skill of its own. Thermals rise over sun-hot rock, so the [Mesa](Biomes/Mesa.md) and the [Scorching Desert](Biomes/Scorching-desert.md) are good flying country by day. Wind drives air up the faces of ridges, and the [Mountain Range](Biomes/Mountain-range.md) can be flown for hours by a rider who knows where the lift is. Sinking air over cold water and the still air of night make flight heavy work.

A flyer must land to rest, eat, and recover, and it will never fly itself to the point of falling. A mount pushed past its tells goes down on its own wherever it can, so a rider who ignored the signs is set down in country they did not choose. Long journeys by air therefore become chains of landings: known ledges, clearings, and hilltops where a flyer can come down, rest, and launch again. A landing place is only worth anything if the flyer can leave it, so heavy run-up flyers string their routes along cliff edges and long open meadows, while a standing flyer can use places they cannot. Those places become valuable knowledge for a flying rider, worth recording on a map or keeping secret, and they are exactly where an enemy who knows the route waits.

## What a Flyer Carries

A flyer carries its rider and little else. Light saddlebags are the limit for most flying mounts, and every kilogram aboard slows the climb and shortens the time in the air. An overloaded flyer refuses to take off at all. Bulk trade in ore, grain, timber, and armour therefore still moves by caravan and ship, as [Economy and Trade](Economy.md) depends on, while flight carries what is light and urgent: a rider, a message, a scout's report, a pouch of mana crystals from the [Floating Islands](Biomes/Floating-islands.md). Only a mana-borne flyer can carry real weight, because its lift does not come from wings, as [Mana-Borne Flight](#mana-borne-flight) describes.

## Weather and Altitude

The sky is weather's own country, and [Weather, Time, and Seasons](Weather-time-and-seasons.md) acts on flyers more than on anything else. A storm grounds every winged flyer. Lightning, hail, and violent turbulence are things no mount will fly into, and a flyer caught aloft when a storm builds turns for the nearest ground on its own, so the rider's job is to find somewhere better to land before the animal settles for somewhere worse. Fog takes away the landmarks a rider navigates by, leaving only a compass and the risk of flying into a hillside unseen.

Height has a price for rider and mount alike. The air grows cold as a flyer climbs, and the exposure rules of [Survival](Survival.md) apply in full to a rider sitting still in a freezing wind, so a high crossing needs the same clothing and preparation as a high pass on foot.

Above the highest peaks and the floating islands the air also grows too thin to breathe. Rider and mount both begin to run out of breath under the rules of [Survival](Survival.md#breath), and the higher they climb, the faster it goes. Flying creatures are built for the high air and hold out far longer than any rider, so the rider always fails first. A rider who climbs past their own warning signs passes out and slides from the saddle, because the harness can hold a braced body through any manoeuvre but not a limp one. The mount goes on climbing for as long as its rider asks, until its own breath runs short. Then it turns down on its own, and even a strong bond only delays that moment. Only a mount in panic, fleeing upward from something it fears more, ever climbs until it blacks out, and a flyer that blacks out falls like a stone. Thin air is the true roof of the sky. It stops a rider from simply climbing over every storm, every mountain range, and every pursuer.

Each flyer has a ceiling above which it cannot climb, and reaching the [Floating Islands](Biomes/Floating-islands.md) takes a mount strong enough to get there. The seasons narrow the flying day, too: winter brings short light, bitter cold aloft, and frequent storms.

## Mana-Borne Flight

The [Flying Leviathan](Creatures/Flying-leviathan.md) does not fly the way the creatures above do. Its body is held up by mana, the same force that keeps the [Floating Islands](Biomes/Floating-islands.md) aloft, gathered in the crystals along its throat, spine, and fins. Its wing-fins and tail fluke drive it forward and its pectoral fins steer it, but none of them hold it up. Mana-borne flight therefore runs on rules of its own. Most of what limits a winged flyer does not touch it, and something else limits it instead.

Staying aloft costs a mana-borne flyer almost nothing. It never tires from holding itself in the air, never stalls, never needs to land, and can hang almost motionless over one spot, which no winged flyer can do. With no launch and no landing, it is boarded and left in other ways: by climbing across from high ground it drifts past, by flying over on a mount of one's own, or by gliding down from its deck on a wing-rig. It cannot dive either. It has no wings to fold and is far too vast, so its only burst of speed is the surge described in its bestiary entry. A pilot steers it with the same reins and intent as any mount, but it answers slowly, turning wide like a ship under sail.

What it spends in place of stamina is mana. The crystals in its body hold a reserve, and everything beyond its unhurried drift draws on it: climbing, surging, holding a line against the wind, and above all carrying weight. Because its lift does not come from wings, a leviathan can bear structures, stores, and people that would ground any other flyer, but every tonne on its deck keeps drawing on the reserve for as long as it flies. A lightly built leviathan can surge and climb for a long time. One laden with a fortress drifts lower and slower and has little left for an escape. Its deck is built with the ordinary pieces of the [Building System](Building-system.md#bases-on-creatures) within a fixed piece budget, and its keepers learn to weigh every wall against the range it costs.

The reserve refills from the world. A leviathan draws mana from the leylines it passes over, which is why its migrations follow the shifting mana flows of the world and why drifting along a leyline does for it what a tailwind does for a winged flyer. In mana-poor country, far from any leyline, the reserve refills only slowly. A drained leviathan cannot surge or climb and sinks to a low, labouring drift, though an adult never falls; that inborn attunement is what separates it from a calf, which dies if it leaves the islands, as its [bestiary entry](Creatures/Flying-leviathan.md#young-and-adult) describes. A pilot reads the reserve through the creature itself. A full leviathan's crystals burn bright and pulse in time with a strong song, while a drained one's crystals dim, the pulse slows, and the song sinks low. A route for a leviathan is planned along the leylines, much as a winged rider plans a chain of landings.

Wind and weather reach it differently too. Wind does not tire it, but its enormous body catches the wind like a sail: a strong headwind can hold it almost still over the ground at its slow drift, and a crosswind pushes it sideways unless it spends mana to hold its line. A storm cannot ground a creature that never lands, so a leviathan either climbs above the weather or rides it out, which drains its reserve and throws everything on its deck about. Its ceiling is set by its mana rather than by thin air, and a full leviathan climbs higher than any winged flyer. It endures thin air longer than anything else that flies, like a whale holding one great breath, and it can carry everyone on its deck into air that will knock them out. A passenger who blacks out on the deck collapses there rather than falling, but a pilot who blacks out leaves the leviathan to its own drift.

A leviathan cannot be stabled, so it is kept a different way while its keeper is away. Before logging out, the keeper anchors it over a leyline of their choosing, and the leviathan circles there, drinking from the current beneath it. Feed stocked in troughs on its deck holds its bond steady for as long as the troughs last, just as a stocked shelter does on the ground. When they run dry, the bond begins to fray, and in time the leviathan slips its anchor and drifts back into its migration, carrying its base with it, so a keeper who stays away too long comes back to an empty sky and a search. An anchored leviathan is still a base, and like any base it can be raided while its keeper sleeps, by anyone who can reach it from the air. Raiders who land on the deck can loot what they find there, but harming the leviathan itself makes it climb. It rises into thin air that it can endure long after anyone on its back has blacked out, which leaves raiders senseless on the deck of a creature that will not come down until the threat is over. Killing one to stop that climb is a huge undertaking and carries the lasting cost to standing set out in its [bestiary entry](Creatures/Flying-leviathan.md).

## Falling

Falls from height kill. A rider who is knocked from the saddle, whose harness fails, or whose flyer dies in the air falls with nothing to stop them unless they wear a wing-rig and there is height enough to open it. That makes a glider the natural companion of a flying mount, the one piece of gear that turns a fatal fall into a hard landing. A rider who passes out in thin air falls with no hand free to open one, but the air thickens on the way down, and a rider who falls from high enough can come to with a few seconds left to open the rig before the ground arrives. A rider who dies aloft, or whose body reaches the ground after a fall, leaves a corpse wherever it lands under the rules of [Death and Loss](Death-and-loss.md). A flyer that dies takes its packs down with it, and the carcass can be looted where it crashes. A flyer whose rider is lost behaves as any mount does in [Creature Riding](Creature-riding.md#when-the-rider-falls), staying near the body or heading home according to its bond.

## The View from Above

From the air a player sees more, but nothing is marked for them. The world from the air looks as it does from a hilltop, only wider, with no markers, labels, or minimap to read it for the rider, as [UI and HUD](UI-and-HUD.md) establishes. A flying rider still navigates by landmarks, rivers, and a compass. A cartographer can sketch the broad shape of country from the air, its coastlines, ridges, and rivers, but the detail that makes a map valuable under [Cartography](Skills/Artisan/Cartography.md), such as dungeon entrances, resource nodes, and the paths between them, is charted on the ground. From the air a valley's shape can be seen; what lives in it cannot.

Flight also gives up concealment. A flyer is a silhouette against open sky, visible from kilometres away, as [Dynamic Culling and Render Distance](Dynamic-culling-and-render-distance.md) is built to show, and the [Birds](Creatures/Birds.md) below lift as it passes and mark its track for anyone watching. Whoever flies trades secrecy for speed.

## The Sky in Conflict

In a fight the rules of mounted combat in [Creature Riding](Creature-riding.md#fighting-from-the-saddle) carry into the air: the rider fights from the saddle, the mount fights as itself, and both can be brought down by whatever reaches them. A rider can also drop what they carry. A firepot, a rock, or a weighted net let fall from the saddle lands hard, but a flyer's light load holds it to a few throws per flight, which makes it harassment rather than bombardment. A leviathan's crew can drop far more from its deck. The heaviest fire from the sky is a [Royal Dragon](Creatures/Royal-dragon.md)'s own breath, poured onto whatever its rider turns it against.

Flyers can strike at a base from above, landing on its walls or inside its yard to let riders down where the gate cannot stop them. They carry too little to bring siege engines with them, so an air assault puts fighters inside rather than breaking walls, and a leviathan drifting low over the walls can put a whole raiding party down at once. Defenders answer the sky by building rather than with dedicated weapons, as the [Building System](Building-system.md#damage-and-siege) describes. Flight carries no immunity in the safe cities either, whose guards keep the same law in the sky as on the streets ([Conflict and PvP](Conflict-and-pvp.md#safe-cities-and-the-reach-of-the-guard)).
