# Creature Riding

A mount here is a living animal with a will of its own, and riding one is a partnership rather than a set of controls. The rider decides where to go and how fast; the creature decides how to put its feet down, what it is afraid of, and whether an order is worth dying for. This document sets out how that partnership works in the saddle: the controls, the gaits and their costs, the refusals, the care a mount needs, fighting on creatureback, and what happens when the rider falls. How a creature comes under a player's control in the first place, and how that control is kept, belongs to [Taming and Control](Taming-and-control.md); flying mounts follow everything here and add the rules of [Flight](Flight.md).

## Reins and Intent

Riding uses direct control of heading and pace. The rider steers with the reins and chooses a gait, and the mount carries that intent out in its own body: it picks its footing on broken ground, steps around a stump or a sleeping dog without being asked, slows itself on a steep descent, and keeps to firm earth at the edge of a bog. Nothing about the rider's input is vague or delayed, so the first ride feels responsive and readable. What the rider does not control is the animal's judgement about danger. A mount keeps its instincts, and above all its will to survive, so it will balk at a cliff edge, refuse to swim water too deep for it, and shy from a charge into something far stronger than itself.

Bond strength sets where those limits sit. A well-bonded mount answers the reins promptly, holds a gallop through smoke and noise, and follows its rider into danger it would never enter alone; a mount whose bond is fraying answers late, drifts toward what it wants, and refuses sooner. The same order can therefore succeed on one animal and fail on another, and a rider learns their own mount's limits the way a person learns a friend's temper.

## Reading the Animal in First Person

The game is played entirely from the rider's own eyes, as set out in [UI and HUD](UI-and-HUD.md), so most of what a rider knows about their mount comes from what sits in front of them. The head and ears are the main instrument. Ears pricked forward mean attention, ears flicking back mean the animal is listening to something behind, and ears pinned flat mean fear or anger. The neck tightens and the head lifts when the creature is uneasy. Breathing grows loud and ragged as stamina runs down, the rhythm of hoofbeats or pawfalls changes with the ground, and every stumble carries through the saddle into the view. The minimal HUD adds a mount stamina indicator only when it is pressing, in keeping with the interface's rule of showing what the moment needs and nothing more.

A refusal never comes out of nowhere. Before a mount balks it gives its tell: ears go back, the head comes up, it sidesteps or slows on its own. A rider who reads the tell has a moment to choose: ease off, calm the animal, or press on and risk the refusal. Only if pressed does the mount stop dead, rear, or wheel away. Fear builds the same visible way. The scent of a large predator, fire, lightning, the crack of loud spellcraft, and the smell of blood all raise it, and a frightened mount grows harder to steer until it either settles or, on a weak bond, bolts with or without its rider.

## Gaits, Stamina, and Ground

Every mount moves in four gaits. A walk is quiet and sustainable, the pace at which a mount recovers its breath and can snatch grazing along the way. A trot covers ground steadily at little cost. A canter is the travelling pace for a long day on a road. A gallop is a sprint: fast, loud, and expensive, draining stamina quickly and leaving an exhausted mount that will drop to a walk and refuse to run again until it has recovered. Riders save the gallop for escapes and charges.

Ground shapes every gait. Roads and open grassland are where mounts are fastest, forest and scrub slow them, and scree, ice, bog, and deep snow force the animal to pick its way, so a rider who demands speed on bad ground invites a stumble, a thrown rider, or an injured leg. Noise matters as much as speed. A galloping mount is heard well before it is seen, its rhythm carries through the ground to the [Sandworm](Creatures/Sandworm.md) in the desert, and a column of riders at the canter announces itself to anyone on the road ahead. A mount also tires over a long day beyond its stamina, much as a character does under the fatigue of [Survival](Survival.md), and a mount ridden from dawn to dusk without rest starts the next day short of its best.

## Kinds of Mount

Mounts fall into broad roles defined by what a creature's body can do, and no single animal is at home everywhere.

- **Riding mounts** carry one rider and modest packs quickly over long distances. The horse is the archetype: wild horses run the [Plains](Biomes/Plains.md), and trained ones stand in the stables of the realms' towns and cities.
- **War and hunting mounts** fight alongside their riders. A [Wolf](Creatures/Wolf.md) raised from a pup is fast, quiet in forest, and will bite at what it is set against, but it carries less than a horse and tires sooner on open ground.
- **Pack beasts** trade speed for load. A [Large Buffalo](Creatures/Large-buffalo.md) hauls far more than any riding mount and is the backbone of a caravan, but it is slow, and it will not cross ground it does not trust.
- **Colossal mounts** are steered rather than ridden. A [Titan Turtle](Creatures/Titan-turtle.md) carries a saddle structure built under the [Building System](Building-system.md) and is guided by hauling its head round with chains, and a [Flying Leviathan](Creatures/Flying-leviathan.md) carries a whole deck aloft.
- **Flyers** carry a rider into the air and follow the additional rules of [Flight](Flight.md).

A creature's role decides which tack fits it and how much it can bear, and a few tamed creatures, such as the [Four-Armed Monkey](Creatures/Four-armed-monkey.md), are companions that can never be ridden at all.

## Hired Horses

No settlement sells a mount. Owning one always means taming one, but nobody has to walk everywhere until they have. The stables of towns and great cities hire out trained horses for a fee and a deposit, and a hired horse can be handed in at any stable of the same realm, so a player can ride from one town to the next and leave the animal there. The deposit comes back on return, less a charge for any wear the horse has taken. The horse is only lent, and its bond belongs to the stable that trained it. It is steady on roads and in ordinary country but timid in a crisis, balking sooner than a well-bonded mount and bolting at the first serious fright.

That makes the hired horse the honest baseline of mounted travel: dependable on a road between settlements, a poor partner on the frontier, and no use at all in a fight. Independent frontier settlements, outlaw towns, and bandit camps stand outside the realms' stable network and will not take a realm's horses in, so a hired horse can carry a player toward the frontier but cannot be left there. A horse kept past its hire is a stolen horse, and the stable's settlement treats it that way in the [Reputation System](Reputation-system.md). If its rider dies, a hired horse makes its own way home to its stable, which keeps a share of the deposit for the trouble. A player who wants a horse of their own tames one from the wild herds of the plains.

## Care and Tack

A mount needs food, grazing on its own when it walks over good pasture and eating from its rider's stores where pasture is poor, so a ride across the [Scorching Desert](Biomes/Scorching-desert.md) or the [Tundra](Biomes/Tundra.md) means carrying feed as well as rations. It takes wounds in fights, falls, and stumbles, and an injured mount is a slower one: a lame animal cannot gallop, and a wound left untreated worsens. Remedies from [Crafting](Crafting.md) and rest treat injuries, and they heal fastest in [shelter](Building-system.md#shelter-for-creatures). A mount that dies is gone for good.

Tack is what lets a mount do its work. Every mount needs a bridle and reins to be ridden, a riding saddle for comfort and seat over a long day, a pack saddle to carry anything beyond its rider, and a war saddle and barding to fight in. Tack is crafted to fit the body it goes on, so a horse's saddle does not fit a wolf, and colossal mounts carry saddle structures rather than saddles. Mount storage follows the same gear-driven rule as the character's own [Inventory](Inventory.md): a mount has no carrying capacity beyond the bags and frames strapped to it, and everything it bears counts against its strength. An overloaded mount slows, tires fast, and refuses to run, and barding adds protection at the cost of speed and stamina. Tack wears with use and weather and needs mending before it fails.

## Fighting from the Saddle

Mounted combat is a full part of the fighting described in [Combat](Skills/Combat.md). A charge turns momentum into force: a couched spear or lance landing at the gallop strikes with a weight no blow on foot can match, but it lands once, and the rider has to wheel away and gather speed again before it can land a second time. Swords and axes work from the saddle at closer range. Bows and crossbows can be shot from a mount, steadiest at a halt or a walk and increasingly wild at speed until horsemanship training steadies them. Spells can be cast from the saddle, though a loud or bright spell frightens a mount that is not used to it.

The mount fights too, in its own way. A rider gives an attack intent, and the creature carries it out with the body it has: a wolf lunges for legs and throats, a buffalo or a turtle tramples and shoulders through, and a dragon fights as itself. Whether a mount will charge at all is a question of bond. A well-bonded war mount runs at a shield wall, while a weakly bonded one balks at the sight of levelled spears, and every rider learns not to plan a charge their animal will refuse. Infantry has real answers to horse. Braced spears and pikes punish a frontal charge, a heavy blow or a hit to the mount can unhorse a rider, and a mount that rears in fear throws whoever is not sitting deep. An unhorsed rider takes the fall, lands on foot among enemies, and has to recover their mount or fight without it.

## When the Rider Falls

When a rider dies, their own body is a corpse under the rules of [Death and Loss](Death-and-loss.md), and the mount's packs follow the mount. A creature with a strong bond stays close: it guards its rider's body and will turn on whoever comes to loot it, or it makes its own way home to its shelter. A creature with a weak bond panics and goes wild. A mount that bolts or heads home carries its cargo out of the fight with it, while a mount that stays to guard keeps its packs beside the body, and a mount that dies leaves a lootable carcass with everything strapped to it, under the same full-loot rule as its rider. Highwaymen therefore learn to kill or cripple the pack animal before they deal with its rider, and a caravan's survival can turn on the courage of its beasts.

A killer can never simply take a mount over. A creature whose rider has died keeps its old bond, which frays as it would through neglect, and the original owner can find it and win it back. Anyone else who catches it has to tame it from nothing through one of the methods in [Taming and Control](Taming-and-control.md), and coercion is the quickest route but also the least reliable. A mount left without shelter when its player logs out stays in the world like the sleeping character described in [Death and Loss](Death-and-loss.md#logging-out). It can be killed or stolen there, and its bond weakens with time away, so the end of a session is also a decision about where the animal spends the night.

## Calling and Leaving a Mount

A mount is always somewhere in the world, and its rider has to know where. A rider can leave it standing, tell it to wait, or let it follow at a distance, and a whistle calls it back from within earshot, provided its bond is good enough to answer and there is a path it can travel. A mount told to wait outside a ruin is a mount alone in hostile country, exposed to predators and passers-by, and a whistle cannot fetch an animal that is a kilometre off or penned in a stable behind walls. Where the mount is, and who might find it there, is always part of the plan.

## Horsemanship

Riding well is a skill, part of the taming and handling disciplines of [Skills](Skills.md), and it grows with time spent in the saddle. A practised rider sits deeper and is harder to unhorse, calms a frightened mount faster, catches its earliest tells, holds a steadier aim at speed, and gets a cleaner charge out of a war mount. Horsemanship never overrides the animal. It makes the rider a better partner, and the bond still decides how far the creature will go.

Across a player's life the arc is a long one. The first hour in the saddle is a hired horse on a road between two towns. Later comes a horse of their own tamed from a wild herd, and after that whichever life they choose: a war mount trained to hold its nerve in a charge, a pack train that turns a trader into a merchant house, or a turtle carrying a fortress across the plains. At the far end lies the sky.
