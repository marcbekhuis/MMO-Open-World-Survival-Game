# Quest System

## Overview

Quests are how the settled world speaks to the adventurers who work its edge. The realms in [Setting and Lore](Setting-and-lore.md#the-age-of-adventurers) now depend on adventurers to open a frontier that has beaten every settler before them, and the quest system is the form that dependence takes in play: [NPCs](NPCs.md) with real needs, in real places, offering work that only someone willing to leave the farm belts can do. A farming hamlet wants its road cleared of a predator, a mining outpost wants an escort for its ore, a city merchant wants a rare component from beyond the last milestone. Completion can grant experience, currency, equipment, materials, unlocks, and reputation, but the stronger purpose is to pull players into the frontier and give their actions context.

Quests are also the delivery vehicle for the authored layer of the world's stories, the regional, faction, and discovery threads the setting describes. They sit beside the emergent story that players make by building, claiming, losing, and defending ground, and never replace it. There is no main quest that every character must walk, and nothing in the quest log is required to play the game well.

## Quest Types

Gathering quests ask players to collect herbs, ore, rare components, salvage, or creature materials from specific environments. They suit explorers, crafters, and small groups, and they teach where resources lie while giving reasons to travel through dangerous territory. The further from safety the source, the better the pay.

Monster hunting quests focus on defeating creatures, bosses, or disruptive packs. They range from solo predator control near a village to coordinated hunts against the regional threats that hold frontier ground, such as a [war-host](Creatures/Red-goblin.md) camped on a trade road. Rewards favour combat progression, trophies, materials, and local reputation.

NPC escort missions send players across dangerous routes with merchants, messengers, pilgrims, refugees, or specialists. These quests test route planning, defensive positioning, dialogue choices, and the ability to react when creatures, bandits, weather, or rival players interfere.

Structure defence quests ask players to protect outposts, villages, caravansaries, gates, shrines, or strongholds. They work best when the defended place has visible value: a blacksmith who will reopen after the attack, a bridge that keeps trade moving, or a settlement whose reputation rewards improve if it survives.

Event quests cover world invasions, seasonal celebrations, faction wars, natural disasters, resource booms, political incidents, and world bosses. These quests temporarily reshape travel routes, settlement services, market prices, and player priorities.

Most of what a player finds to do on any given week is systemic. Contracts, bounties, convoys, gluts and shortages, camps that regrow, and the wars players make among themselves refresh on their own and need no writer. The authored layer arrives with the content rhythm set out in [Monetization and Platforms](Monetization.md#the-content-rhythm): each update of roughly three months brings a handful of hand-written arcs, one or two for the cities and the newest reach of the frontier, together with the creatures, dungeons, and events they lean on, and the yearly frontier expansion brings a larger arc tied to the land it opens. Authored quests are written to be few and worth finding rather than many and disposable, because the systemic world carries the volume.

## Quest Rewards

Rewards scale with difficulty, distance, risk, and social consequence.

| Reward Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Experience** | XP for levelling and progression |
| **Currency** | Gold or coin for trading and purchasing |
| **Equipment** | Weapons, armour, accessories |
| **Materials** | Crafting components and resources |
| **Unlocks** | Rare abilities, dyes, finishes, clothing |
| **Reputation** | Faction standing and NPC relationships |

## Dynamic Quest Availability

Quest availability depends on location and world state, and it changes with distance from safety in the same way the land does in [World Design](World.md#leaving-the-farm-belts). Urban settlements and the heartland around them produce trade requests, local disputes, merchant support work, and service recovery quests. The farming hamlets at the edge of the farm belts post the nearest dangers: a predator on the road, a blight in the stores, a patrol that did not come back. Out on the frontier the work turns to hunting, gathering, exploration, survival, and rescue, offered by trapper camps, mining outposts, and the independent settlements nobody else will serve. Sea and sky regions support ocean travel, piracy, merchant encounters, flying creature hunts, and island colonisation, while [dungeons](Structures/Dungeons.md) and caves focus on recovering what the world's currents have grown inside them, exploring their depths, driving out what dwells there, and treasure hunting.

Dynamic events interrupt the normal rhythm. A storm can strand traders, a faction war can close a road, a resource boom can draw miners and raiders, and a world invasion can pull nearby settlements into a shared defence.

## Quests in a Persistent World

A single-player game can write a quest as a private story in which the player is the one who matters. A world shared by thousands of adventurers cannot, and the quest system is built on three consequences of that.

Repeatability separates the two layers of quest. Systemic quests are contracts, bounties, deliveries, and defence calls that settlements generate from their own needs and the state of the world around them, and they refresh as those needs return: a road that is cleared is raided again, a stockpile that is delivered is drawn down, a bounty is posted for the next beast that takes its place. They carry the bulk of play and never run out, because the world keeps producing the conditions that create them, as the [Economy](Economy.md) keeps producing prices. Authored arcs are the opposite: short, hand-written chains of quests that belong to the individual character, tell a particular story about a particular place, and are completed once by each player who takes them up. Neither is a substitute for the other, and a player can live on either, or on neither.

Shared world state means that what one party does in the world is true for everyone. A goblin camp that a group clears stays cleared, for every player passing that way, until the goblins return to it on the world's own clock, and a beast that is hunted down is gone from its territory until its kind repopulates it. A quest to clear such a camp is therefore a standing opportunity rather than a private instance: the first group to answer the call does the work, the contract is fulfilled, and the next adventurers find the road quiet and the settlement grateful. Where two groups reach the same target at once, the world resolves it by who gets there, as every other contested resource does. The regrowth that restores a camp, a lair, or a settlement's population follows the same persistence rules as the rest of the world, set out in [Server Architecture](Server-architecture.md#8-shared-world-state) and [Settlement Generation](Settlement-generation.md#residents-and-repopulation).

There is no chosen one. The player is one adventurer among many thousands, all of them answering the same call, and no quest tells a player that they alone can save a settlement, defeat a mythic power, or decide the fate of a realm. Authored arcs centre instead on local people and places: a hamlet whose elders hide why the last settlers left, a mining outpost whose foreman is stretched past breaking, a shrine whose keeper knows what lies beneath it. The player is a capable outsider who arrives, helps, and moves on, and the story is that of the people and the ground. The larger stories of the frontier, which realm holds which pass and what becomes of a war-host, belong to what the players do together, and the [Reputation System](Reputation-system.md) records how settlements and realms remember it.

## At Launch

At launch the quest system is systemic first. Settlement contracts, bounties, and the situations drawn from the bestiary's story hooks fill every settlement from its first day, and each of the three starting cities carries a short authored arc that introduces its realm, its people, and the nearest stretch of frontier. Both layers are scoped alongside the rest of the game in [Launch Scope](Launch-scope.md), and the authored layer grows afterwards as the frontier opens further.
