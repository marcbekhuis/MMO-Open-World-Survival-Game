# Death and Loss

Death in this world is a setback with teeth, not a slap on the wrist and not the end of a character. Every trip beyond a safe city's walls is a wager, and what a player stands to lose turns above all on whether the wilderness or another player is the one who brings them down. This document sets out what happens at the moment of death, what is at risk, and how a player gets back on their feet, and it is the rule set referred to wherever the rest of the design speaks of what a defeated player loses.

## The Corpse and What Is at Risk

When a character falls, they leave a lootable corpse holding what they were carrying: the contents of the bag, pouches, and hand slots described in [Inventory](Inventory.md), down to and including equipped gear. Carrying capacity is therefore also exposure, and the weight a player hauls into danger is precisely the weight they can lose. The one reliable exception is the mage's [Item Box](Magic/Spells/Misc/Item-box.md), whose stored contents sit outside the body entirely and never appear on the corpse, which is part of what makes the spell so prized; an item counts as stored only once the short deposit cast has finished, so a mage cut down mid-cast drops what they were trying to save. Goods banked in a settlement or a player's own base are never at risk from a death in the field; only what is carried is. A base is a different matter, since it can be raided whether or not its owners are there to defend it, as [Raids and Conquest](Raids.md) sets out.

What death never takes is who the character has become. The levels, stats, and use-earned skills set out in [Player Progression](Player.md) are untouched, because progression here is the record of what a player has done and is not something the design strips away on a bad night. There is no permadeath.

## Dying to the World

A death to a creature, a fall, the cold, or any other hazard of the wilderness is meant to sting and then be survivable. The corpse persists for a recovery window, and a player can make the run back to reclaim what they left, so the true cost of a lonely death in the wilds is the dangerous return trip, the wear every death puts on equipment, and the handful of perishables and loose resources the world claims in the meantime. Whatever still lies on the corpse when the window closes is gone for good, destroyed rather than left for anyone, which makes an abandoned corpse one of the economy's steady sinks. This is the moderate, recoverable outcome that lets the wilderness punish carelessness without erasing an afternoon's work, and it is why a careful player treats a corpse run as the second half of a bad fight rather than a lost cause.

The catch is that the world is not the only thing that may find the body. In contested country a corpse left on the ground is an opportunity for any player who happens past, so even a death to a wild beast can become a total loss if someone reaches it first. Distance from safety raises the stakes of every death, by design.

## Dying to Another Player

When the killer is another player, the gloves come off: the victor may strip the corpse completely. This is full loot, everything carried and everything worn, and it is the central stake of the open world. It is the reason the frontier's first rule is never to carry what you are not prepared to lose, the reason a laden merchant or a richly geared raider is a target worth hunting, and the engine that keeps gear genuinely valuable, since anything that can be won can also be lost. What changes hands this way does not vanish from the world but recirculates through it intact, feeding the markets of [Economy and Trade](Economy.md); only the wear of the death itself and whatever the victor leaves behind to the closing recovery window are lost.

Full loot is deliberately harsh, and the design does not pretend otherwise. Its counterweights are the safe cities and [reputation consequences](Reputation-system.md) set out in [Conflict and PvP](Conflict-and-pvp.md), the player's own freedom to decide how much to risk on any given outing, and the storage systems that let wealth be banked rather than worn into a fight.

## Logging Out

A character does not leave the world when its player does. Outside the guarded safe areas of NPC cities, a character whose player logs out or loses their connection stays exactly where it stood, asleep, for as long as the player is away, and it can be found, killed, and looted like any other body. A death in that sleep leaves a corpse under the rules above, and the player wakes at their respawn point when they return. Inside the safe area of an NPC city, as defined in [Conflict and PvP](Conflict-and-pvp.md), the character leaves the world entirely and is beyond anyone's reach until its player comes back.

That gives every player a clear choice at the end of a session. A player who wants the full stakes of the open world can stop wherever they are and trust their hiding place, their base, or their friends to keep them alive while they sleep; a player who wants their character safe makes the trip back to a city first. Logging out is never an escape from a fight, because the body stays behind.

## Getting Back on Your Feet

A fallen player respawns at a bound safe location, by default the [great city](Structures/Great-cities.md) they are tied to, or a bed or claimed point established through the [Building System](Building-system.md) so that groups working the frontier can return closer to the action. From there the choice is immediate and familiar to the genre: re-equip from storage and cut the losses, or gear up and race back to the corpse before the window closes or a stranger arrives. Either way the loop turns again, a little wiser about what to bring next time.
