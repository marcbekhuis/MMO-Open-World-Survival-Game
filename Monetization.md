# Monetization and Platforms

How the game is sold and where it runs are design decisions as much as business ones, because both shape who plays and how the persistent world is sustained. The short version is deliberate: a single subscription funds everything, nothing else is ever for sale, and the game is built first for PC with consoles kept in view for later. This document sets out the business model and platform direction and the principles that keep them aligned with the rest of the design.

## A Single Subscription

The game is funded by a subscription and nothing else at the level of access. There is no box price to clear before a player can begin and no content walled off behind separate purchases; one recurring fee opens the whole world and pays for what a persistent online world genuinely costs, the live operations, the always-on infrastructure described in [Server Architecture](Server-architecture.md), and the steady stream of new content that keeps the world growing. Tying the game's income directly to whether players are content enough to keep paying, month after month, aligns the studio's interest with the players': the only way to earn is to keep the world worth living in.

The subscription costs €12,99 or $14,99 a month, with three-, six-, and twelve-month plans that bring it down to about €10,99. That is the price the genre's long-lived subscription games have settled on: [World of Warcraft](https://eu.shop.battle.net/en-us/product/world-of-warcraft-subscription) and the standard plan of [Final Fantasy XIV](https://eu.finalfantasyxiv.com/product/) charge the same, and [Mortal Online 2](https://www.mortalonline2.com/press-kit/), the closest full-loot neighbour, charges slightly more on top of a box price. [EVE Online](https://www.eveonline.com/news/view/plex-and-omega-price-changes), the oldest sandbox still running on subscriptions, has raised its monthly price above that line to cover rising costs. Matching the anchor rather than undercutting it keeps the price unremarkable, and dropping the box price that Mortal Online 2 charges removes the one barrier a newcomer would otherwise meet before seeing the world.

The subscription is the only thing the game ever sells, and that holds through the staged release set out in the [Launch Scope](Launch-scope.md). Closed tests are free and by invitation. Early access charges a reduced monthly price, which rises to the full €12,99 on the day the wipes end and the persistent world begins. The reduction is what players are owed for wipes and an unfinished world: they pay less while the world can still be reset beneath them, and the full price begins only when what they build is kept for good.

## What the Subscription Must Carry

The server design is expensive by intent, so the price has to be checked against it. A world's cost has two parts. Compute follows the crowd rather than the map, because quiet regions are consolidated onto a single [Cluster Unit Server](Server-architecture.md) and empty land costs almost nothing to keep running; only populated regions demand servers of their own. Bandwidth follows the players themselves, and at the update rates the design needs it is the larger of the two. Public cloud prices put compute at well under a dollar per concurrent player each month and bandwidth at one to three dollars, which with persistence and operations comes to roughly one and a half to four dollars per concurrent player. Long-running subscription games see somewhere between one concurrent player for every five subscribers and one for every eight, so the infrastructure behind each subscriber costs well under a dollar a month, below a tenth of the price. The rest pays for the team that keeps the world growing, which is the real standing cost of the model, and the per-player bandwidth budget in [Dynamic Culling & Render Distance](Dynamic-culling-and-render-distance.md) is treated as a commercial limit as much as a technical one.

## The Content Rhythm

A subscription promises that the world keeps growing, and the promise needs a rhythm the studio can staff. A content update arrives about every three months, carrying new creatures, spells, authored quest arcs for the cities, dungeons, and systems moved from post-launch into the game in the order the [Launch Scope](Launch-scope.md) sets out. Once a year a major update opens a further reach of the frontier, newly finished ground that brings a new biome with its creatures, resources, and dangers, so that the world's own story of a frontier pushed back year by year is told through the calendar. This sits between the patch cycle of [Final Fantasy XIV](https://ffxiv.consolegameswiki.com/wiki/Patches), a little over four months, and the monthly updates of [Rust](https://rust.facepunch.com/news/surviving-12-years/), close to the four-month major updates [Albion Online](https://mmobomb.com/news/albion-online-will-shorten-guild-seasons-drop-3-major-updates-year-starting-2025) has moved to. Between updates the world carries itself: systemic contracts, the economy, settlements repopulating, and the wars players make of their own accord supply most of what changes from week to week, and the authored layer is the seasoning on that, not the meal.

## No Pay-to-Win, by Design

Because the subscription already funds the game, nothing else needs to be sold, and nothing else ever is. There is no store of any kind, cosmetic or otherwise. Appearance comes from the world instead: dyes, finishes, and clothing are crafted, found, or traded, and can be lost, so how a player looks is something earned and risked like any other possession, and never an edge or a status bought with money. Nothing touching combat strength, loot, carrying capacity, territory, or the pace of progression is offered either. In a full-loot world where wealth and power are won and lost through play, as set out in [Economy and Trade](Economy.md) and [Death and Loss](Death-and-loss.md), letting either be bought would hollow out the very stakes the design is built on. Every advantage traces back to what a player did, never to what they paid.

That promise has to be defended, because a full-loot economy is a magnet for people who sell in-game wealth for real money. Gold farmers and bots will come, and a staged death is the easiest way to hand a buyer a corpse full of goods without any trade on record. The game sells nothing that turns money into coin, not even game time traded between players, so every route from real money into the world is illegitimate and treated that way. Buying or selling coin, items, or characters for real money is a permanent ban for the seller and every account linked to them, and the buyer loses what was bought, down to a negative purse if it has already been spent. Detection is a standing operation, not an occasional sweep: the server already records who looted whom, so repeated one-sided kills between the same accounts, goods flowing from new characters to established ones, and gathering at a pace no person keeps are all signals it watches. A fresh account brings nothing with it either: a new character starts with worthless starter equipment and no coin, as [New Player Experience](New-player-experience.md) sets out, so a throwaway account is worth only the labour put into it. The economy is shaped against farming as well. Because an NPC vendor's price for a good has no floor, as set out in [Economy and Trade](Economy.md#how-npc-prices-move), a farmer cannot dump one resource into a market for steady coin, and the sinks that drain wealth from everyone drain it from the farmer's stockpile too. The studio has no illusion that this ends the black market; it makes it expensive, slow, and risky enough that buying wealth is never the sensible way to get it.

## Platforms

Development targets PC first. The keyboard-and-mouse control surface suits the depth of the systems, the hardware ceiling suits the photorealistic fidelity described in [Art Direction](Art-direction.md), and an open platform leaves the most room for a community to grow around the game. Consoles are kept deliberately within reach rather than ruled out: holding to the Mature standard set in [Content and Tone](Content-and-tone.md), with no explicit content to bar certification, keeps the door to console ports open for after launch, when the interface and control work in [UI and HUD](UI-and-HUD.md) can be extended to suit a gamepad properly instead of bolted on as an afterthought.

## Who It Is For

The game is built for adults who want a deep, harsh, persistent world rather than a quick session and a soft landing. Its player accepts that the frontier can take everything they carry, measures their investment in weeks and months rather than minutes, and finds the reward in mastery, territory, reputation, and the kind of stories only a world with real stakes produces. Every choice in this document, the single fair price, the refusal to sell power, the PC-first build, follows from taking that player seriously.

That player is a niche, and the design counts on it being one. Full loot narrows the audience and a subscription narrows it again. The games that do both, [EVE Online](https://www.eveonline.com/) and Mortal Online 2, keep thousands rather than hundreds of thousands playing at once, while free-to-play Albion Online reaches several times that and box-priced Rust, with neither filter, reaches more than ten times it. Even so, EVE has sustained a studio for two decades on a subscription sandbox. The game is planned to close at tens of thousands of subscribers, not millions: fifty thousand subscribers at the monthly price bring in close to eight million euros a year before platform fees, enough for a focused team and the infrastructure above, and the seamless world and its server model scale up without redesign if the audience proves larger.

A player with a few evenings a week is welcome in that world, because losing gear is meant to sting rather than to end a week. The everyday kit a player takes onto the frontier, sound mid-tier armour and weapons, can be replaced in an evening of gathering and crafting or bought with an evening's trade, while the finest gear stays expensive enough that risking it is a real choice. A full-loot death therefore costs a night's work for a player who went out in everyday gear and a great deal more for one who chose to carry their best, which is the same calculation [Death and Loss](Death-and-loss.md) asks every player to make before they leave the walls.
