# Horse

The horse is the archetype of the riding mount: a big, deep-chested grazer of the open grassland that a rider first meets on loan from a stable and later, if they are patient, as a wild animal they have won for themselves. In the wild it is neither monster nor prey that fights back, but a herd animal built around flight, and the whole of its character lies in the gap between what it can do for a rider and what it will do when it is afraid. It outruns every predator of the [Plains](../Biomes/Plains.md) while its wind lasts, it carries a rider and modest packs further in a day than anything else a player is likely to tame, and it is the creature the rest of the [Creature Riding](../Creature-riding.md) rules are most often written around.

## Appearance and Visual Design

A horse stands 1,50 metres at the shoulder, runs about 2,40 metres from nose to rump with a further 0,75 metres of tail, and weighs between 450 and 550 kilograms. A stallion is the larger animal of a herd, about a tenth taller and heavier than the mares, with a thicker crest to the neck and a broader chest. A foal stands 1,00 metre at the shoulder at birth and weighs about 60 kilograms. The body is long-legged and deep through the girth, with four limbs, each ending in a single uncloven hoof, a long arched neck carrying a head of about 0,60 metres, and a mane and a tail of coarse long hair that move with every stride and show the wind and the animal's temper at a glance.

Wild coats run bay, dun and grey, in a rough order of how common they are. Bay is a warm brown with a black mane, tail and lower legs; dun is a sandy yellow-brown with a darker stripe along the spine; grey is a dappled iron colour that whitens with age, so an old grey in the herd is nearly white. Every horse has paler muzzle and belly hair, and many carry a white blaze on the face or a white sock on one or two legs, which is how a rider tells one animal from the next across a field. Foals are born in spring in a pale, fuzzy version of the adult coat with legs that look too long for the body, and they lose the fuzz by midsummer. In winter every coat grows shaggy and dull, the mane thickens, and the breath hangs in the air around the herd (see [Weather, Time, and Seasons](../Weather-time-and-seasons.md)). The eyes are large and set on the sides of the head, and the ears are the most expressive feature of the animal: pricked forward for attention, swivelling for sound, flat to the skull for anger or terror.

## Movement and Posture

A horse grazes with its head down and its weight on three legs, lifts its head every few seconds to look and listen, and walks at 1,8 metres a second between mouthfuls. It trots at 5,0 metres a second, a steady gait it can hold for a long time at little cost, and canters at 8,0, the travelling pace of a long day on a road and already faster than a sprinting player's 6,5. Its gallop is 13,0 metres a second, faster than the [Wolf](Wolf.md)'s 9,5 and the [Red Deer](Red-deer.md)'s 11, and it holds that for about 14 seconds before it is winded, against the wolf's eight and the deer's five. Over a short run it escapes everything on the plains, and over a longer one it still outlasts the wolf, which is why a healthy adult horse is almost never taken by a pack. The foal, the old animal, the lame one and the horse that has been galloped to the end of its wind are the ones that die. A winded horse drops to a walk, flanks heaving and nostrils flared, and cannot be pushed faster until it has recovered, as [Movement](../Movement.md#stamina) sets out for every winded body.

Its body is heavy for its speed. A horse turns in place slowly, swinging its quarters round, and at a gallop it runs wide, so a rider cannot haul it round on the spot and a chase across open ground is decided by lines rather than by reflexes (see [Turning and Facing](../Movement.md#turning-and-facing)). A canter on hard ground carries a long way, and a gallop is heard well beyond sight and felt through the soil.

A horse sees to 50 metres, within 150 degrees either side of where its head points, which leaves a blind wedge of 60 degrees straight behind it and a smaller blind patch directly in front of its nose, within about a metre. Its head turns toward what it watches by at most 90 degrees, so the wedge behind swings with the head, and an animal that is merely grazing is blind across the 60 degrees at its tail. It hears to 70 metres, further than a wolf, and its ears track a sound before its head does. It smells a downwind approach from much further still. A person who walks up on a grazing horse from straight behind and downwind, or who stands still within its blind patch, can stay unnoticed until it turns, but a person who walks in from the side in the open is seen at the edge of its range, as [Noise](../Movement.md#noise) describes for the way a walking, jogging and sprinting body is heard.

## Herd and Behaviour

Wild horses live in herds of eight to twelve: a lead mare, a stallion, the other mares, their foals and a few yearlings. The young stallions are driven out when they mature and run in bachelor bands of three or four until they are strong enough to take a herd of their own. The lead mare, the oldest and steadiest of the females, chooses where the herd grazes, when it drinks and when it moves, and she is its first sentry. When she lifts her head and stops chewing, the herd stops with her, and when she turns and goes, the others turn at once. The stallion is the herd's guard rather than its guide. He grazes at its edge and moves around it, keeps to the side the wind is not carrying scent from, and drives any straggler back into the group with ears flat and neck stretched low.

The herd reads a person on foot by distance. At about 40 metres the lead mare lifts her head, the others follow, and all turn their ears to the stranger, which is the warning window, and a player who stands still here is often only watched. At 25 metres the herd walks away, keeping its distance, and at 15 metres or at the instant any horse is struck, startled or hurt, it breaks into a gallop that keeps together behind the lead mare, the foals running inside the bunch and the stallion last. A rider on a horse of their own is read differently: wild horses are less alarmed by another horse, and a rider who comes up at a walk can come to within about 20 metres before the lead mare decides.

A herd that smells or sees wolves bunches tight, foals into the middle, and the stallion places himself between the pack and the group, ears pinned and neck snaking. If the wolves come closer than about 30 metres, the lead mare leads the herd away at a canter, and at a closer approach or at the first lunge it bolts at a gallop. The stallion does not run until the herd is clear, and he will wheel and strike with his forefeet and kick at a wolf within reach of his body. A pack that sees a healthy stallion standing his ground tends to leave the herd alone and look for the foal that has fallen behind, for a horse in the open with its herd around it is a poor target. A herd that scents fire or catches sight of a [Highland Dragon](Highland-dragon.md) does not stand at all: it runs straight away from the smoke, in the open and in one direction, as the [Large Buffalo](Large-buffalo.md) herds do, and what it leaves behind is the lame animal and the foal that could not keep up.

## Tells and Temper

A horse that is frightened or angry says so before it acts. The ears go flat to the skull, the head comes up and the eyes show white at the rims, the nostrils flare and the animal snorts, and the tail clamps or lashes. A horse that is about to bolt first stamps, sidesteps and backs away with its head high, a tell that lasts perhaps a second and a half before the gallop, and the same tell is the one a rider feels through the reins on a mount that is losing its nerve, as [Creature Riding](../Creature-riding.md#reading-the-animal-in-first-person) describes. A horse that is about to kick turns its rump to the thing it dislikes, pins its ears and lifts one hind hoof clear of the ground for about half a second, and the kick reaches about 1,5 metres behind the animal. A horse that is about to rear drops its head, sinks on its hindquarters and plants its forefeet for about a second before the front rises and the forefeet strike. A person who walks behind a horse that is giving the first sign, or between a mare and her foal, finds that the tell has already ended.

A horse almost never attacks to kill. Cornered, it kicks and rears, and a stallion defending his herd will close the distance to strike, but at any chance the animal runs, and a player who gives it room is never the target. A wounded horse that cannot flee is dangerous mainly to what is standing behind it.

## Taming and Keeping

All three bonds of [Taming and Control](../Taming-and-control.md) work on a horse, and each shows its character here. Slave bonding is possible, but a horse roped and beaten is a strong animal that panics: it bolts, rears and kicks until it breaks free, and a handler who holds it by force alone is rarely in the saddle long. Parental bonding is the strongest route, a foal raised from the weeks in which it still suckles, and the bond that grows from it is deep enough for a war mount that stands in a charge. Trust bonding is the common route for a rider with patience: the handler feeds a wild horse day after day at the edge of its herd, starting from a distance the animal will tolerate and closing a little at a time, and only one handler can win a given horse at a time. A horse that has been won this way and is well bonded is a far steadier mount than a hired one, which carries its stable's fixed bond and is timid in a crisis, as [Hired Horses](../Creature-riding.md#hired-horses) sets out.

A horse takes a bridle and a saddle made to fit its body, and nothing else: the riding, goods and armour saddles of [Saddles and Carriages](../Saddles-and-carriages.md) all exist in a horse's size, and a horse is also the animal most often put to a carriage. A hired horse is lent for the saddle only and is never put to the harness, so a carriage team is always a team its keeper has tamed.

A tamed horse counts as sheltered when the four conditions of [Shelter for Creatures](../Building-system.md#shelter-for-creatures) are met. It wants a stocked trough of hay or grain within reach, a roof overhead, and a stall of at least three metres by three so that it can stand, turn and lie down. Its climate need is the cooling that heat-sensitive creatures need: it is hardy in cold, and shade and a source of cool air matter more to it than a hearth, which is why a plated horse in the [Scorching Desert](../Biomes/Scorching-desert.md) is a horse in danger. While its trough is stocked it keeps its bond exactly as it was, and without shelter its bond weakens through [neglect](../Taming-and-control.md#bonds-while-away) like any other animal's. It eats from its rider's stores on a road where pasture is poor, and it comes to water at dawn and dusk like any grazer (see [Survival](../Survival.md#creatures)).

## Drops and Uses

A dead horse yields a great deal of meat, a thick hide, sinew and bone, and the long hair of the mane and tail, which is twisted into rope and bowstring and woven into cloth. The meat spoils as described in [Survival](../Survival.md#food-and-hunger), and the carcass is butchered in parts over a long time, with the exposure that brings, as [Gathering](../Gathering.md#hunting-and-butchering) sets out. A wild horse is rarely worth killing for its meat alone, because the animal that matters to a player is the one that is alive, and a horse that dies under a rider is gone for good along with everything strapped to it.

## Story Hook

A drovers' camp on the plains has lost eleven horses over three nights, each time with the picket ropes cut clean and the camp dogs silent. The tracks leave in a bunch and keep to the hard ground, shod hooves among the unshod, so the herd was ridden away and not stampeded. In a dry gully a day downwind the foals stand alone around a dead mare, their manes freshly cropped, and the trail runs on toward a camp that no realm's stables answer for. A player who follows it has to decide between bringing the foals back to the drovers, keeping one to raise as a mount of their own, or riding on after the thieves before the herd is sold.
