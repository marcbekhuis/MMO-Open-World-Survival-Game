# Populations and Spawning

Every creature in the world is a particular animal rather than a stand-in drawn up when a player walks past, and once it exists it stays until something in the world kills it. The wilderness is filled through spawn zones: stretches of ground that each know which creatures belong there, in what proportions, and how many the land can hold. A zone brings in new animals only when its ground has been hunted below what it should carry, and the bulk of every population's recovery comes the way it does in nature, from breeding and raising young. Creatures are not tied to a fixed spot. They roam, rest, and hunt wherever their nature takes them, so a base raised in a meadow or a wall run across a valley changes where they go rather than trapping them in place. This document describes how creatures and settlement people come to be where they are, how they persist, and how they recover, so that the wilderness can carry the consequences of what players do to it.

## Every Creature Is Someone

A creature is a persistent individual. Its record holds its species, its sex, when it was born, its health and wounds, where it stands, and the group it runs with. That record is the creature; the body a player sees is only its view. While the ground beneath it is loaded, the creature exists as a full body in the world and is never removed. When the ground unloads, because no player has been near it for a while, the body is written back into the record, and when the ground loads again the same animal stands up where it was left. Time has passed in between, and it is applied at once by each rule below: wounds have healed, the young have grown, a calf may have been born, and an old animal may have died. This is the same rule the [Server Architecture](Server-architecture.md#8-shared-world-state) applies to everything the world does unobserved.

Between those two states sits a third. A creature on loaded ground with no player anywhere near it is **dormant**: it does not think, move, or send anything to players, and it costs the server almost nothing, yet it is still there. A group wakes when a player comes within a distance comfortably beyond the reach of its senses, so that it is already grazing, pacing, or watching by the time anyone could notice it, and falls dormant again only once every player has gone well beyond that distance. The wake distance is the group's senses plus a margin of 30 metres by default, checked about once a second. Waking does not spread on its own. Only a group that a player has woken can wake another, and only one step deep: a hostile group a player woke wakes any group within its own wake distance, which is how a pack drawn out by a passing hunter still finds the herd in the next valley, and that herd wakes nothing further. A sleeping creature never counts as a threat that keeps another group awake, so a herd lying within sight of a sleeping pack sleeps on beside it, and a region falls quiet again once the players have left it. Larger creatures wake from further away, in step with how far they can be seen, as [Dynamic Culling and Render Distance](Dynamic-culling-and-render-distance.md) sets out for drawing them.

The consequence is a world that remembers. A hunter who drives a spear into a pack leader and escapes finds the same pack ranging the same woods two days later, one wolf fewer if the wound was mortal, the leader still scarred if it was not. A herd scattered by wolves at dusk is still scattered at dawn. Scouting pays, because what a scout saw is what the party will meet.

## Spawn Zones

The world's wild ground is covered by **spawn zones**, each a stretch of land with a list of what lives there. Every entry on the list names a species or a kind of group, a herd of deer or a pack of wolves, with a weight that sets how often it is chosen and a ceiling on its share of the zone, so that a forest zone fills mostly with deer, now and then with a wolf pack, and never with wolves alone. Each zone also has a carrying capacity, the number of animals its ground can feed, and a minimum below which it calls in new arrivals. Zones overlap and nest freely: a broad forest zone can hold a smaller one along a river where boar and waterfowl gather, and a cave mouth can have a zone of its own. How many zones a biome holds and what their lists contain follows the biome's own character, as [World Design](World.md) and the individual biome files describe it.

Every group belongs to one zone at a time: the zone whose ground it last stood on among those that list its kind, and the smallest of them where zones nest. A wolf pack that crosses a meadow whose list holds only deer stays the forest's pack. A herd that drifts from one valley into the next stops counting toward the first and starts counting toward the second, so the land is reckoned by what is actually on it, and a predator that follows its prey across a ridge thins the zone it hunts in. A group that wanders onto ground no zone covers still counts toward the zone it left, and so does a group whose ground has unloaded, so no zone ever replaces animals that are merely out of sight, and the world as a whole never holds more than its zones can carry. A tamed creature leaves the wild count altogether. No limit caps how many creatures a player or a guild may keep; what bounds a stable is the upkeep [Taming and Control](Taming-and-control.md) asks of every kept creature, in feed, shelter and the keeper's time, so a stable full of mounts never empties the valley around it of wild animals, and taming a herd's last stag does not stop the land from carrying new ones.

Each entry also says what its creatures may appear on. Most wild entries name the ground itself, down to the layers of the landscape: deer appear only where the terrain is meadow grass or open woodland floor, a lizard only on bare rock, a wader only in shallow water, so a zone painted across mixed ground still puts each animal where it belongs. Other entries name built places instead, such as the inside of a building, the yard of a camp, or a road, which is how a bandit camp gets its sentries and a ruin its squatters. Whatever the surface, the point must be walkable for the species, not too steep, and clear of player structures and claims by a good margin, unless the entry asks for a built interior.

Nothing is ever spawned in sight of a player. A point is acceptable only when no player can see it, by distance or because the land or a wall stands in between, and a zone that finds no such point waits and tries again later rather than forcing one. The rule holds for everything that appears from nowhere: a body that returns as its ground loads does so only because no player is near enough to see it. A birth is the one thing a player may witness, because it does not appear from nowhere. The mother leaves the group's line, lies down and labours, and the young animal is there at her side, wet and unsteady, before it finds its feet and follows her; an egg cracks and the hatchling works its way out. A species with no such birth to show gives birth only out of sight.

## Free Roaming

Creatures belong to their zone only in the sense that it brought them into the world. A group ranges where its species ranges, rests where it finds cover, and moves on when its ground stops serving it. When players build across a group's favourite clearing, wall off a pass, or burn a stand of trees, the group finds somewhere else, and wolves that once laired among the roots of an old wood might den on the far side of the ridge once a settlement grows up beside it. No creature waits behind a wall for a home it can no longer reach, and no base becomes unusable because a herd insists on standing in it.

Roaming is still bounded by nature. A species keeps to the kinds of ground it is described on, so deer stay on grassland and open woodland, and a [Wolf](Creatures/Wolf.md) pack dens in temperate woodland and ranges its edges, which makes the [Forest](Biomes/Forest.md) the place most players meet one. Animals pushed out of their kind of ground by a fight or a chase drift back toward it once the danger has passed.

## Breeding and Growth

Populations recover mainly by breeding. A group that holds an adult male and an adult female, in a zone below its carrying capacity, conceives now and then at a pace set for each species, and the young are born after the species' gestation, the same durations [Taming and Control](Taming-and-control.md) uses for bred companions. Egg-laying species lay in nests of their choosing instead, and the eggs hatch as warmth allows. A birth that falls due while nobody is near happens when the ground next loads, with the young animal already as old as the time since its birth; one that falls due in front of a player plays out as the birth described under [Spawn Zones](#spawn-zones).

The young follow their mother and grow into adults over a span set for each species. Size, strength, and speed come with age, so a yearling stag is a smaller, slower, gentler thing than the animal it will become, and predators know it: the wolves that close on a deer which falls behind its herd close fastest on the young. A young animal still inside its parental window is the one a handler can take for [parental bonding](Taming-and-control.md), which makes a herd's birthing season worth watching and a den's pups worth the risk of approaching.

## Age and Death

Every creature ages, and every species has a natural span with some variation from animal to animal. An old animal slows before it dies, and an old animal is the first a pack takes, so predators keep herds young and herds keep predators fed. A creature that reaches the end of its span dies where it is; if nobody is near, the death is settled when the ground next loads, and the population simply has one member fewer. When a group's leader dies, by age or by spear, the strongest adult remaining takes its place.

Age is what keeps a long-running world fresh. Without it, the same adults would hold the same ground for as long as the server ran, every rare animal would be found and camped, and every zone would sit at its capacity untouched. With it, populations turn over across the seasons, new leaders rise, and a valley's herds in the second year of a world are not the herds of the first.

## The Minimum and New Arrivals

Breeding cannot rebuild a population that has been wiped out, and a world in which a single determined party can empty a region for good is a world that runs out. When a zone has fallen below its minimum and stayed there through a quiet period, it calls in new arrivals: it draws groups from its list by their weights and places them, one by one, at unseen points on open ground, until it holds its minimum again. The arrivals keep away from the places where the zone's animals have recently died, a memory that outlasts the quiet period, so a hunter who waits where the last herd fell waits for nothing, and the newcomers turn up somewhere across the zone instead.

A zone's total can hold while one of its species has gone, and a species that is gone breeds no more. An entry can therefore carry a minimum of its own, which watches over its species across the surrounding country rather than inside the zone alone: as long as enough of that species lives within reach of the zone, whether on its own ground or a neighbour's, the entry rests. Only when the species has thinned out over the whole surroundings does the zone call in a group of it. A herd that wanders into the next valley is therefore never replaced behind its back, and a small zone at the edge of a species' range needs no floor at all, since its neighbours keep the species alive.

The minimum is a backstop, not the engine of the world, and it sits well below the carrying capacity. Clearing a stretch of forest of its wolves, or a cave of its [swamp spiders](Creatures/Swamp-spider.md), buys the area a real reprieve: the newcomers are few, and the population returns to its full strength only by breeding, over weeks rather than hours. Settlers and hunters who want a valley quiet have to keep it quiet.

## Settlement People

The people of settlements follow the same persistence. Every resident is a specific person with a home, a post, and a routine, kept as a record while no player is near and brought into the world as a full character when one is, as [Settlement Generation](Settlement-generation.md#residents-and-repopulation) describes. A resident is never brought in on an open street in front of a player: they enter the world indoors, at home or at their workplace, wherever their routine has them inside, and step out into the street from there. Residents are not drawn from spawn zones, but the people who belong to no settlement are: the sentries of an outlaw camp, the squatters in a ruin, a band of travellers at a roadside fire, each from an entry that names the built place or the road it uses.

Settlement residents do not breed. A settlement recovers from its losses through newcomers arriving on its own clock, at a pace set by its food, its safety, and its housing, and that rule plays the part the minimum plays for creatures. Children appear only in safe routines, in line with [Content and Tone](Content-and-tone.md), and never as a population that can be thinned.

## Load and Server Settings

Dormancy is what lets the world keep every creature without paying for every creature. The servers spend their effort where players are, as [Server Architecture](Server-architecture.md#9-scale-and-running-cost) intends, and the cost of a region follows the players in it rather than the animals that live there. Each region also has a creature budget, a hard ceiling on the animals it holds whatever its zones would carry, tamed creatures included. At the ceiling, births and new arrivals of wild animals in that region wait until deaths have brought it below, so a crowded stable can fill a region and hold the wild increase back; no living creature is removed to make room, and no tamed creature is ever taken from its keeper. The zone capacities remain the ordinary limit, and the budget is the safety valve that keeps a misjudged zone, or a server whose settings were turned up, from costing more than a region's server can afford. Under heavy load the distance at which ambient wildlife wakes is the first thing to shrink, in the shedding order [Server Settings](Server-settings.md#hotspot-workloads-and-degradation) defines, while hostile creatures within reach of a player stay awake at every stage.

The kill-site memory is a setting of its own and must always be the longer of the two periods. A zone falls due for new arrivals at the moment its quiet period ends, so a memory that merely matched the quiet period would forget the deaths that emptied the zone just as it came to place newcomers, and a herd could appear where the last one fell.

Every number this system uses is an overridable default per species and per zone, as [Server Settings](Server-settings.md#official-and-community-servers) requires of all balancing: the pace of breeding, gestation and growth, the natural span, each zone's carrying capacity, minimum and list weights, each entry's own minimum and how far around the zone it looks, the quiet period before new arrivals (one minute by default), how long a zone remembers where its animals died (five minutes by default) and how far new arrivals keep from those sites, the margin added to a group's senses for its wake distance (30 metres by default) and each region's creature budget. A community server that wants a teeming, fast-turning wilderness can have one, and an official server can keep its animals rare.
