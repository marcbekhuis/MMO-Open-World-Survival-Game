# Prototype Findings

This is a working file, not part of the design proper. It collects what the companion Unreal Engine 5.8 prototype, `MmoSurvivalGame`, has learned by building the design: places where an idea did not survive play, needed a number the documents do not give, or turned out to work better another way. The prototype reads this document as canon and never edits the design files on its own; instead, every session in it records its findings here, and the findings move into the design documents and their `(Technical)` companions once the user accepts them. See [AGENTS.md](AGENTS.md) for how this file fits the wider workflow, and [Design-backlog.md](Design-backlog.md) for questions the design itself still has to answer.

## How entries work

Each finding sits under the system it concerns, newest last. An entry names what the prototype tried, what play or testing showed, and the change it proposes, with links to the documents that change would touch. The date is the day the prototype established the finding, and the code path points at the prototype module where it can be seen. An entry stays here until its change has been written into the design or the user has rejected it; then it is removed, so this file holds only what is still waiting for a decision.

## Building System

These findings come from the building prototype's first slice, the core loop of placement, snapping, stability, and collapse, built against [Building System](Building-system.md) and [Building System (Technical)](<Building-system (Technical).md>). Prototype module: `Source/MmoSurvivalGame/Building/`.

**Every foundation touches the ground (11 October 2026).** The worked example in *A Wall Raised Step by Step* lets a second foundation hang over falling ground, held by its neighbour until a pillar beneath it reaches the terrain, and the technical guide's ground rule (section 3.3) allows the same. In play this produced foundations floating off ledges and made the ground skirt meaningless. The prototype now refuses any foundation that does not find the ground under all four corners and its centre within its skirt, so every foundation is a stability source; the joint that let a foundation rest on a pillar top is gone. The proposal is to rewrite the worked example around a foundation that steps down the slope on its own skirt, and to drop the held-by-a-neighbour case from the technical guide and its validation table.

**A pillar is a source only on terrain (11 October 2026).** The technical guide makes a pillar a ground source when its foot rests on terrain or on a foundation. A pillar standing on a foundation, or on a ceiling, could then restart stability at full strength halfway up a tower, which lets a builder stack storeys without limit. In the prototype a pillar is a source only when its foot is on the terrain; anywhere else its foot is an ordinary vertical joint. The proposal changes the pillar rule in the technical guide, section 3.3, to match.

**Snap compatibility belongs to the pair, not the point (11 October 2026).** The technical guide gives every snap point an `Accepts` list and a joint kind of its own. Building the kit showed that the joint kind depends on what meets what: the top of a wall is a vertical joint for a wall stacked on it and a lateral one for a ceiling laid across it. The prototype holds a single table of pair rules in the piece registry, each naming two snap types and the joint they form, matched hierarchically through the gameplay-tag tree so a handful of rules covers the kit. The proposal replaces the per-point `Accepts` and joint fields in the technical guide's data model with that pair table.

**Snap points are not occupied (11 October 2026).** Tracking which snap points are taken proved unnecessary. The prototype lets a snap point accept any number of links and prevents double placement by testing the new piece's shrunken core box for overlap with its neighbours, which also catches a second ceiling slid into the same span from another edge. The technical guide can drop snap occupancy from its records in favour of the overlap test.

**The worked example's numbers (11 October 2026).** With dressed stone losing 20 stability over a vertical joint and 350 over a lateral one, rough timber losing 60 and 250, and the bands set at blue from 900, green from 600, yellow from 350, and red below, the example reproduces as written in unit tests and over the network: stone foundations at 1000, the wall at 980, the first timber span at 730 in green, the second at 480 in yellow, and the outer span back to 750 in green once the far pillar lands. A pillar under a ceiling forms a lateral joint, which is what returns that span to green rather than blue. The proposal records these values in the technical guide as the reference tuning for its validation case.

**Raising a free-standing foundation (11 October 2026).** On flat ground every foundation sat flush, which left no way to lift a floor off wet ground or level a terrace. The prototype lets a free-standing foundation rise up to 1 metre above flush in 10-centimetre steps, inside its ground skirt so it still touches the ground and stays a source; anything snapped to it inherits the height through the snap. The proposal adds the raise to the placement rules in [Building System](Building-system.md) and its range to the technical guide.

**Snap points must be visible outside the piece (11 October 2026).** Markers drawn at the snap point itself sat inside the pillar and could not be told apart, so a builder could not choose between a wall meeting a post in its middle or flush at either corner. The prototype draws each marker just outside the piece's surface as a small arrow along the joint, colours the three pillar-face positions differently, and names the highlighted point on the screen. The proposal adds this to the build-mode presentation in [Building System](Building-system.md) and [UI and HUD](UI-and-HUD.md).

## Wolf and Creature AI

These findings come from the wolf-pack prototype, built against the [Wolf](Creatures/Wolf.md), the [Tell](Glossary.md) entry in the glossary, and section 7 of [Taming and Control (Technical)](<Taming-and-control (Technical).md>), and play-tested in first person against packs of five and four wolves. Prototype module: `Source/MmoSurvivalGame/Creatures/` and its `Wolf/` folder.

**The tell must come from the edge of view (18 October 2026).** The [Wolf](Creatures/Wolf.md) makes the dropped tail and low shoulders the player's cue to turn and brace, and the natural reading — the wolf deepest in the player's blind spot attacks — puts that tell behind a first-person camera where nobody can see it, so the knockdown becomes unavoidable. The prototype chooses its lunger from the band between 50 and 110 degrees off the player's view direction, at the edge of vision, and only falls back to a wolf outside the band when none is in it. Facing the lunging wolf within 45 degrees braces: the bite still lands for half its force, but there is no knockdown. The proposal states in the wolf entry that the lunge comes from the corner of the eye, and names facing the wolf as the brace.

**Attacks come in waves of feints and lunges (18 October 2026).** A single lunge every few seconds made the pack feel passive, and a player who did nothing was left standing among idle wolves. The prototype sends a wave every 1,5 to 3,5 seconds once the circle has closed: the one or two wolves the player is looking at dash in, snap, and peel away, and while the player is fixed on them a third wolf gives the tell and lunges from the side. Once six or more wolves are fighting, a second lunger follows a second later from the opposite flank. One wolf always holds back, so a wave never empties the circle. This is the [Wolf](Creatures/Wolf.md)'s "press from the sides while a player is fixed on the wolf in front" made concrete, and the proposal writes the wave into its *Pack Tactics*.

**Closer, faster, and never still (18 October 2026).** A pack that watched from 18 metres and closed its circle from 15 to 5,5 metres over twelve seconds read as hesitant, and a wolf that only trotted could not keep up with a running player. The prototype watches from 12 metres, closes from 10 to 4,5 metres in six seconds, trots at 4 metres a second, and sprints at 9,5 metres a second for about eight seconds, against a player who jogs at 3,5 and sprints at 6,5 (the movement module's pace, which replaced the 6 of the first tests). A wolf whose pool is empty is winded and drops to a recovering gait: a walk of 1,5 metres a second while it is calm, and the trot, at its slower recovery, while it is under stress (see *Stress is on for any wolf near its prey*). The [Wolf](Creatures/Wolf.md)'s "outruns a running player over short distances before it tires" holds at those numbers, but only against a sprinter. Scripted runs on a dedicated server with 150 milliseconds of lag showed the difference. A player who jogged away from a stalking pack of five for a minute was caught by wolves that closed at the trot and sprinted in short bursts, and was knocked down twice; between them the wolves sprinted for about a minute and the most tired fell to 1,7 of 100 without going winded, so a jogger does not wear a pack out. A player who sprinted round in the open drew the same five wolves into 66 seconds of sprinting in a hunt of 55, and a third of their samples were winded. Stalking wolves take cover behind trunks and rocks near their place in the circle and pace about it, and a wolf the player walks straight at gives ground sideways, swinging round towards the player's back, so a pack the player charges splits and flows round instead of retreating in a line. The proposal adds the distances and the flowing retreat to the wolf entry, and states in [Movement](Movement.md#creatures-and-mounts) that a winded wolf walks only while it is out of danger.

**The leader is the easiest wolf to hit (18 October 2026).** The leader holds the front and fixes the player with its gaze, which also puts it in front of the player's spear. With the pack breaking when the leader fell below half its health, two spear thrusts ended every fight. The prototype breaks the pack only when the leader has lost three quarters of its health. The proposal gives "wounding the leader breaks the pack's nerve" that weight, a serious wound rather than a scratch, and asks whether the leader should also hang a step further back than the circle, as it does in the prototype at 1,3 times the circle's radius.

**A howl does not summon rival packs (18 October 2026).** "A howl from any wolf calls nearby wolves into the fight" first pulled a second, fully led pack into the hunt, which put two leaders and nine wolves round one player and blurred whose morale held. In the prototype only leaderless wolves answer — the remnant of a pack whose leader fell, or a lone wolf — and they fight as followers of the pack that howled. A pack with its own leader ignores another pack's howl and will not hunt prey that another pack is already hunting. The proposal says which wolves a howl calls in the wolf entry.

**A broken pack leaves (18 October 2026).** A pack whose nerve broke fled, then hovered at watching distance in plain sight, which looked like confusion rather than fear. The prototype has a broken pack, or one that has fled within the last minute, withdraw to about 30 metres from any player it sees, keeping to the trees, and the same holds when a pack meets a party larger than itself. The proposal adds that withdrawal to the wolf entry's paragraph on abandoning a fight.

**Fire slows the hunt without ending it (18 October 2026).** A torch that made wolves keep their distance entirely would make the player immune, while a light the circle ignored would mean nothing. The prototype gives a torch a 6-metre light and a campfire an 8-metre one, each with a further 2 metres in which wolves still feel it. Fear grows while a wolf stands in that zone and settles when it leaves; a frightened wolf backs out of the light and will not attack until it has calmed, and enough fire sends it running. In play the circle paces at the edge of the light and attacks far less often, which is the hesitation the [Wolf](Creatures/Wolf.md) describes. The proposal records the radii and the fear thresholds, hesitation at 0,35 and recovery at 0,25 on a scale from calm to panic, in section 7.3 of the taming technical guide.

**A StateTree brain could not be built with the available tooling (18 October 2026).** Section 7.1 of [Taming and Control (Technical)](<Taming-and-control (Technical).md>) hosts each creature's brain as a StateTree. The editor tooling the prototype uses can inspect StateTree assets but not author them, so the wolf's brain is a C++ state machine whose states map one to one onto StateTree states, with every decision in pure functions that unit tests drive. The proposal keeps StateTree as the target and adds to the guide that a species' decisions — which state wins, which slot and which attacker the pack picks — live in plain functions the tree's conditions and tasks call, so they stay testable without a running world.

**Perception on the pawn works (18 October 2026).** Section 7.1 leaves open whether the AI Perception component behaves on the creature pawn as it does on a controller. It does: the perception system casts sight from the owning pawn's eyes, registers the senses whatever the owner is, and only leaves the update binding to the owner. Wolves with perception on the pawn noticed players by sight in single player and on a dedicated server. The proposal closes the prototyping item in section 7.1.

**Bite checks must not read bones on a dedicated server (18 October 2026).** A dedicated server does not refresh the bone transforms of a mesh nobody renders, so a bite test placed at the head bone hits wherever the reference pose left the head. The prototype tests a sphere in front of the creature's body instead. The proposal adds the rule to the guide's creature section: server-side hit tests for creature attacks use the actor's own transform, never animated bones.

**Tells travel as replicated state, not as events (18 October 2026).** A tell is the one animation a player must never miss. Sent as an unreliable broadcast, a dropped packet loses it; the prototype replicates the current action with a sequence counter instead, and a client starts the animation when the value arrives. With a tenth of the packets dropped and 150 milliseconds of lag, the client saw the tell before every lunge. The proposal adds this to the replicated behaviour state in section 7.1.

**Every creature sees every other creature (18 October 2026).** Pawns register as sight targets by default, so each wolf's sight also tracks every other wolf in range, only to discard them, and the cost grows with the square of the creatures nearby. Hearing does the same with footsteps and breath: in a 70-second run five wolves received 1182 footstep and 314 winded-breath stimuli, all from one another. The proposal adds to the server budgets in section 8.2 that creatures and players carry a team affiliation and a creature's sight and hearing detect only what it treats as a threat; the cost itself has not been profiled.

**Stress is on for any wolf near its prey (20 October 2026).** The first idea for a winded wolf was positional: one at the back of a large pack walks to recover, while one still in danger keeps trotting. The prototype turns that into a stress flag. A wolf is under stress when a threat is within 15 metres, when the pack fighting with it is three wolves or fewer, or when it holds a lunge or feint order, and a winded wolf under stress trots at 4 metres a second where a calm one walks at 1,5. The rule never separated front from back. A hunting circle closes from 10 to 4,5 metres and the pack watches from 12, so every wolf in a hunt has the player inside 15 metres, and a round circle puts every wolf at about the same distance from the player whatever its place in the pack. In six scripted hunts with the real numbers 2886 of 3490 half-second samples of the hunting wolves were under stress, and 1150 of the 1152 samples in which a wolf was winded; those wolves trotted. A wolf walked to recover only when it was more than 15 metres out, which in practice meant once the pack had lost its prey. As built, then, an exhausted wolf trots for as long as the hunt lasts. That agrees with [Creatures and Mounts](Movement.md#creatures-and-mounts), where the wolf "has to drop back to a trot to recover", and not with the rule in [Stamina](Movement.md#stamina) that a winded body is held to its recovering gaits, which for a wolf means a walk. The proposal is to say which holds. Measuring danger from the fight rather than from the player would separate the wolves, calling a wolf calm when no wolf holding an attack order or mauling the target is within a few metres, so that the back of the circle walks and the front trots. Otherwise the design can drop the position, let every winded wolf trot while the pack hunts and walk once it breaks off, and state that in [Movement](Movement.md#creatures-and-mounts) and [Wolf](Creatures/Wolf.md). Prototype module: `Source/MmoSurvivalGame/Creatures/Wolf/` (`WolfPackTactics`, `IsUnderStress`).

**A grazing wolf is blind behind it (20 October 2026).** [Noise](Movement.md#noise) says that a player who walks the last stretch may see a [Wolf](Creatures/Wolf.md) pack before it hears them, but neither document says what a wolf sees. The prototype gives a wolf sight to 30 metres within 110 degrees either side of its head, hearing to 50 metres, and a head that turns toward what it watches by at most 80 degrees and only when it has something to look at. A wolf with nothing to watch is therefore blind in a 140-degree arc straight behind it. In thirteen scripted runs the sight sense reported the player 411 times and never from outside that cone (the widest angle was 110 degrees), and wolves with the player 24 metres away and behind them, at head angles of 140 to 175 degrees over several samples, did not notice him until they turned, about three seconds later as the roaming pack walked on. A standing player made no sound at all: the hearing sense reported him in none of three runs where he stood still, while a sprinting player gave the pack about 500 hearing updates in a hunt of 55 seconds. The proposal states the numbers in the wolf entry, sight of 30 metres within 110 degrees, hearing of 50 metres, and the blind arc behind a wolf that has nothing to look at, so that approaching a grazing pack from behind at a walk or a stand is a defined tactic, and asks whether the blind spot beside a wolf that has just leapt past its prey is wanted: at under a metre it loses sight of the player until it turns, and only the pack's eight-second memory keeps him a threat. Prototype module: `Source/MmoSurvivalGame/Creatures/` and its `Wolf/` folder.

## Movement

These findings come from the shared movement module that every walking body in the prototype uses, built against [Movement](Movement.md) and [Movement (Technical)](<Movement (Technical).md>). Prototype module: `Source/MmoSurvivalGame/Movement/`.

**A sprinting cut costs far more than a third (19 October 2026).** Section 4.1 of the technical guide says a person brakes fully and cuts a right angle at a third less speed. With the guide's own player numbers, a 5-metre radius at full sprint, full turn braking and a 45-degree burst cone, the prototype's scripted cut from 6,5 metres a second loses 72 per cent of its speed and needs 0,83 seconds to settle on the new heading, both in single player and over the network with 150 milliseconds of latency. The cone is what dominates: the moment the new direction lies outside it, the sprint is no longer granted and the body drops to the jog's 3,5 metres a second, a loss of almost half before turning has even begun, and the turn braking takes most of the rest. Switching the turn braking off still left 56 per cent. The proposal is to decide which promise wins, the readable "a cut costs a third" or the strict burst cone, and either widen the cone while a sprint is already running (keeping the narrow one for starting a sprint), or let a running sprint keep its speed through the cone for as long as the facing turns at its full rate, then restate the expected loss in the validation table of section 12.

**Replayed moves must keep their own view (19 October 2026).** Section 7 of the technical guide lets the saved move restore the intent and the spend on replay, and section 4.2 relies on the control rotation already travelling with every move. In Unreal Engine 5.8 the saved move's stored control rotation is overwritten with the live view after every replay, so from the second correction on, replayed moves turned the body toward where the player was looking now rather than where they had looked, and a player sweeping the view while sprinting drew a stream of corrections with up to 14 centimetres of position error. The prototype's saved move records the view once, when the move is made, and restores that copy on replay; afterwards the same run showed no stray corrections. The proposal adds the view to the list of what the saved move records and restores in section 7.

**A move that spends does not also recover (19 October 2026).** The stamina sketch in section 5.1 of the technical guide restarts the recovery delay when a move spends, but still applies the gait's recovery rate in that same move, so a walking body that swings a sword refills a little of what the swing cost. The prototype clamps the rate to zero in any move that spends or drains, which the unit tests now pin down. The proposal makes the clamp explicit in section 5.1.

**A glance over the shoulder turns a sprinting body (19 October 2026).** The validation table in section 12 of the technical guide promises that a sprinting player who glances back keeps the line of travel while other players see the head turn. Section 4.2, though, makes the requested view the facing's target whenever the body moves, so a 70-degree glance starts the body turning towards it, the new heading leaves the 45-degree burst cone, and the sprint falls from 6,5 to 2,8 metres a second within a second; in the prototype the line of travel only held because the scripted run kept its input fixed in the world. The standing rule already has the answer: the body turns only when the view leaves the head's reach. The proposal is that a moving body also keeps its facing while the view stays within the head's 80 degrees, and turns only for the part of the view beyond it, so a glance is a glance and a deliberate look round still steers. A related wording point in the same table: a standing flick to look behind settles the facing at 100 degrees, the view less the head's reach, not at 180; what arrives about a quarter of a second later is the camera, after 0,28 seconds in the prototype.

**The view pitch field the guide names is deprecated (19 October 2026).** Section 8 of the technical guide relies on `APawn::RemoteViewPitch` to carry a body's head pitch to onlookers. Since Unreal Engine 5.6 that byte is deprecated in favour of a 16-bit value read through `GetRemoteViewPitch()`, which the prototype uses. The proposal updates the name in section 8.

**A sprint down loose ground stumbles about every other second (20 October 2026).** [Movement (Technical)](<Movement (Technical).md#91-ground>) has the server roll for a stumble each second from the step's risk and the ground's roughness, and its validation table sends a sprint down scree through that roll, but neither it nor [Movement](Movement.md) gives the downhill term or the numbers. The prototype multiplies the gait's risk (0,2 a second for the sprint, 0,02 for the jog) by the ground's roughness (1 on scree and ice) and by one plus the downhill slope in tens of degrees, holds the product at 1, and lets a hit cost 40 per cent of the speed and 0,6 seconds at a walk. On the 25-degree scree slope of the movement course that is a 0,7 chance per roll: twenty scripted descents produced 66 stumbles, and over the last 5,5 seconds of a descent the body covered 22,3 metres, about 4,0 metres a second against 6,4 for a clean sprint and 3,5 for a jog on the same ground. The sprint still wins, but by roughly 15 per cent at a cost of 6,67 stamina a second, and a 38-degree slope would saturate the roll, so a sprint there stumbles at every roll. The proposal is to decide this on purpose: either state the downhill term in section 9.1 and accept that steep loose ground is for careful jogs, which fits how [Creature Riding](Creature-riding.md) describes scree, or soften the term (a smaller factor per ten degrees, or a cap of twice the base risk) if the sprint should stay worth taking downhill. Every correction in the runs followed a stumble within half a second, so the rule that a stumble is never predicted holds at 150 milliseconds of latency.

**Ground and load are one rules table in data, not functions in the solve (20 October 2026).** Section 9.2 of the technical guide has the solve call `LoadRoleCap`, `LoadSpeedFactor` and `LoadHandlingFactor` on the carried share of capacity, which writes the inventory's burden tiers into code, while [section 9.1](<Movement (Technical).md#91-ground>) keeps the ground table as a separate project-wide asset. That cuts against the rule the prototype holds everywhere else, that every balancing number is data a server can override by name. The prototype therefore keeps one rules asset for what all bodies share. It holds the ground table, one row per surface type with a speed factor, a noise factor, a roughness and a role cap, and the load tiers as an ordered list, each giving its upper limit as a share of capacity, a speed factor, a handling factor, a stamina cost factor, the role cap and whether jumping is still allowed. The last tier covers every load above the others, so a tier with a speed of 0 and a very high limit stands an overloaded body still. The design fixes the shape of the tiers but not every number, and the prototype fills the gaps by carrying the previous tier's value forward: up to full capacity speed is 0,95 of normal with a handling factor of 0,85 and stamina costs of 1,1; up to a quarter over it is 0,9, the burst role is gone and costs are 1,5; up to half over only the recovering gaits remain and jumping is refused, with the speed and handling of the tier before; beyond that the body cannot move. The proposal is to rewrite [section 9.2](<Movement (Technical).md#92-load>) around a tier table held in the rules asset, and to add those starting values to [Carrying Weight](Movement.md#carrying-weight) and the burden tiers of [Inventory](Inventory.md#weight-and-strength-mechanics), where only the bands and the direction of each effect are given today.

**A gait's noise is a distance, not a loudness (20 October 2026).** Section 2 of the technical guide gives every gait a unitless `NoiseLoudness`, which [section 9.3](<Movement (Technical).md#93-noise>) multiplies by the ground's noise factor before it reaches the hearing sense, yet [Movement](Movement.md#gaits-on-foot) states noise in metres: a walk is heard at around 5 metres, a jog at 15 and a sprint at 35. Turning the one into the other would need a conversion that lives nowhere in the design and that no tester could check against it. The prototype stores `NoiseRangeCm` on each gait instead, the distance at which that gait is heard on neutral ground (2, 5, 15 and 35 metres for the crouch, walk, jog and sprint), multiplies it by the ground's noise factor (1,2 on forest floor, 1,5 on scree, 0,5 on moss) and gives the result as the range of every footstep event, so a sprint on scree carries 52,5 metres. A winded body adds a range of its own, 8 metres once a second, which is further than the 5 metres of the walk it is held to and so keeps the design's promise that a winded player carries further than their footsteps. In the prototype's listener tests a sprint was heard 30 metres away and not at 40, and a walk at 4 metres and not at 6. The proposal replaces `NoiseLoudness` with a range in centimetres in section 2 and states the metre figures per gait in [section 9.3](<Movement (Technical).md#93-noise>).

**The design gives no acceleration or braking, so the prototype derived them (20 October 2026).** Each gait row in section 2 of the technical guide carries an acceleration and a braking value, and section 4 hands both to the character movement component, but neither the guide nor [Momentum](Movement.md#momentum) states a number; the specification says only that a walk or a jog reaches its pace in half a second and stops within a stride, and that a sprint stops in roughly two and a half metres. The prototype derives the values from those sentences: an acceleration of speed over half a second for the walk and the jog (3,0 and 7,0 metres a second squared, with the crouch taking the walk's), 11,0 for the sprint so that it reaches its 6,5 metres a second in 0,59 seconds, and a braking deceleration of 8,75 for the slow gaits, which stops a jog in 0,70 metres, and 8,45 for the sprint, which stops it in exactly 2,5 metres. These are starting values only. They are computed from the specification's wording and untested in play, so whether a jog that stops in under a metre feels precise or abrupt belongs with the sprint radius and the about-face as a question for the first hand-played session. The proposal is to list them in the profile table of section 2 as starting points, so that the guide stops implying values it never gives.

**Vaulting and mantling need bands and timings the design does not give (20 October 2026, updated 21 October 2026).** [Vaulting and Mantling](Movement.md#vaulting-and-mantling) speaks of walls "up to about waist height" and ledges "between chest and head height", and [section 6.1 of the technical guide](<Movement (Technical).md#61-movement-modes>) plays each traversal "over a duration from the height", but neither gives the numbers. The prototype vaults anything from 0,50 to 1,10 metres, mantles from 1,20 to 2,00 metres, treats a step below half a metre as an ordinary jump, and refuses the band between 1,10 and 1,20 metres outright, which is the gap the two phrases leave between waist and chest. A vault takes 0,5 seconds per metre of height, so a 60-centimetre wall passes in 0,30 seconds and a 1,10-metre one in 0,55, and the body leaves it with four fifths of its speed: a sprint at 6,5 metres a second comes off every test wall at 5,2, which reads as the "without breaking stride" the specification promises. A mantle takes 0,6 seconds plus 0,5 seconds per metre, about 1,3 seconds at 1,40 metres and 1,5 at 1,80, and costs stamina on a straight line from a twentieth of the pool at chest height to a seventh at full reach, 7,3 and 12,0 points at those two heights. The jump has to come within about a metre of the face, roughly 0,15 seconds of a sprint. A burdened body, here anything over its carrying limit, keeps three tenths of its speed over a vault and cannot mantle at all. All of it held over the network at 150 milliseconds of latency without a correction on the course's square walls and ledges. The first hand-played session, on the same day, answered the open question and found three more gaps. The refused band reads as a bug, and so does every other refusal of a height a person could plainly cross: a waist-high platform two metres deep is too deep to vault and too low to mantle, a fence or a thin wall between chest and head height is too high to vault and too thin to stand on, and a ledge just above the 2,00-metre reach of the arms cannot be grabbed, although a player expects to jump, catch the edge and pull up. The prototype has since built exactly that, and on 21 October 2026 a test yard of 64 obstacles, every height from 0,40 to 2,60 metres at four depths plus fences, an overhang, sloped tops, walls on slopes and landings of different widths, passed all 384 scripted approaches, square and at 30 and 50 degrees, from a standstill and from a sprint, at 150 milliseconds of latency without a correction. A top up to 1,10 metres and no deeper than 1,20 metres is vaulted, a deeper one is climbed onto with a hop-up at the chest cost of a twentieth of the pool, a higher top at least half a metre deep is mantled, and a thinner one is climbed over at the mantle's cost. The overhead reach measured from the character model, the shoulder joint's height plus the length of the arm to the tip of the middle finger, came out at 2,18 metres for the 1,80-metre template body, under the 2,25 to 2,35 metres a standing person reaches, because the shoulder rises when the arm is raised and the joint positions of the model's rest pose do not show it; the prototype keeps the measured value, so a 2,20-metre ledge is out of reach, and a tuning value for a jump on top of the reach exists for when play says otherwise. The reach counts from the feet, not from the ground at the face, so a ledge at the top of a slope is no easier to grab than it looks. A climb-over and a vault both need room beyond the obstacle: the body lands only where the whole of its footprint fits, which for the template body means at least 0,70 metres of ground, and a second fence or an overhang closer than that refuses the move. A climb-over refuses a landing more than two metres below the ground it started from, while a vault keeps going and falls, so a waist-high railing at a cliff edge is vaulted and the fall is judged by the ordinary landing rules. A wall approached at an angle is crossed straight over its face, not along the line of the run, up to 60 degrees off square, so a player who runs at a fence diagonally steps across it the short way. The proposal adds the bands, the two duration rules, the kept fraction and the jump window to the specification as starting values, adds the hop-up and the climb-over to [Vaulting and Mantling](Movement.md#vaulting-and-mantling) beside the vault and the mantle with the landing room, the two-metre drop limit of the climb-over and the 60-degree approach, and moves the line where [Climbing](Movement.md#climbing) begins from the reach of the arms to the reach of the arms raised overhead, measured from the body and open to a small allowance for the rising shoulder.

**The server should not take the traversal's starting point from the client (20 October 2026).** [Section 6.1 of the technical guide](<Movement (Technical).md#61-movement-modes>) keeps "the measured obstacle" in the saved move, and the plan behind the prototype read that as the obstacle's height, depth and start transform. Sending the start transform would let a client place the start of a fixed path wherever it liked, which is a teleport by another name. The prototype instead sends only the measured obstacle in whole centimetres and the traversal's clock in milliseconds. The server anchors the path at its own position for that move, measures the obstacle again, and accepts the client's figures only within four centimetres plus one rounding step, measuring for itself otherwise. Two machines that measure the same wall from positions a prediction error apart disagree by a few millimetres, so the tolerance never fired in the test runs; a forged height would be replaced by the server's own, although no run has tried one yet. The proposal names in section 6.1 what the move carries (the obstacle measurement and the progress through the traversal, not where it starts) and states that the server checks the measurement against its own.

**A climb refused for want of stamina still spends a jump (22 October 2026).** [Vaulting and Mantling](Movement.md#vaulting-and-mantling) and [section 6.1 of the technical guide](<Movement (Technical).md#61-movement-modes>) say when a mantle is refused, for full hands or too much load, but not what the jump becomes when the body simply lacks the stamina for the climb. In the prototype the press then falls through to an ordinary jump, which costs its five points as any jump does. In the first hand-played session a player who arrived at a 1,80-metre wall with a little over five points jogged at it four times in eighteen seconds; each attempt was refused at about five points against a cost of twelve, each jump took the pool back to nearly nothing, and the jog's slow refill of 1,1 points a second never let the climb come within reach until the player stood back for twelve seconds. The design's own rule that survival pressure should create decisions rather than chores suggests the refusal should cost nothing: a body too tired to climb stays on its feet, perhaps with a short failed-grab animation that tells the player why. The proposal adds that rule to the stamina paragraph of [Vaulting and Mantling](Movement.md#vaulting-and-mantling) and to section 6.1, and leaves an ordinary jump for presses with no climbable obstacle ahead.
